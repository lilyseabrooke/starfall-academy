So, you’ve found yourself in the hot seat. If you’re looking to GM, this section is here to help out. You won’t find rulings or important numbers below—you can run the system without having read a word in this section—but you might want to study it for some guidance on how to run the best Starfall Academy game you can.

## The World of Starfall

Starfall Academy stands out for its focus on intrigue, mystery, and character-focused dynamics. If you’re used to other TTRPGs that have more dungeon-crawling elements, Starfall Academy might take some getting used to.

Games will often be more focused on roleplay and creative problem-solving in social situations, mystery situations, and confrontations with authority and conspiracies than on hacking and slashing monsters.

That said, there’s totally space for hacking and slashing monsters. It’s just more of an occasional treat than it is the main dish, you know?

When you create a game, you’ll want to immerse your players fully in a magic-academy world. Don’t be afraid to indulge in “fluff” with scenes like a scuffle with a bigheaded rival student between classes, a study group interrupted by an accidental summoned creature, duels between Houses springing from nowhere in the open fields, and spend time in the lush descriptions of the place.

Unlike many adventure-focused TTRPGs, the events in Starfall Academy generally all revolve around one place, which means every interlude scene and passage of rich description helps build the world that surrounds the players all the time.

## Gameplay Sequences

The general course of a game can follow as many paths as you can dream of for a story, but it will help to break the game down into **sequences** with defined objectives on a theme, and those **sequences** tend to follow one of a few paths.

There’s no one formula for a good game, but a reliable starting point is to run **sequences** that are 2-4 sessions in length, rewarding the players at the end of each, both with moving the overall plot forwards and with in-game rewards, like materials, potion recipes, new wands, spell learning materials, or even artifacts.

The main archetypes of the most common **sequences** are listed below, but nothing ever fits a single mold cleanly—most of the time, players will be on a heist with dungeon elements, or solving a mystery through a roleplaying route, and so on. But knowing the general molds will help you form your **sequences.**

### Roleplay

:::quote
***The party*** *needs to get close to the scheming student who they know is behind the necrotic trolls but who has avoided leaving any hard proof.****After getting caught mid-heist****, the party needs to barter and negotiate with the Enforcer Captain whose dark secrets they know more than they’ve been letting on, to convince them to drop the case.****The powerful magic heiress*** *who’s been working with the party is losing herself to doubt, and is being lured onto a dark road by demons, and the party needs to convince her back to the light.*
:::

**Roleplay sequences** are based primarily around interactions between characters, the players talking their way through difficult moments. These sequences typically involve less physical danger and more intangible risks—getting in trouble with the authorities, ostracizing somebody important to the overall plot, advancing the plans of a greater villain, and so on.

These **sequences** tend to be cozier and easier on players’ frayed nerves, so it makes a great option for following up an intense, high-stakes **sequence** like a **Battle** or a **Heist**.

That doesn’t mean these **sequences** should be vague. When you’re putting the players on the route of a **roleplay sequence,** convey very clearly to the players what the mission is and what’s at stake, and what a “victory condition” would look like.

That’s not to say these **sequences** should be stressful, though, just that the goals should be clear. Maybe the stakes are just that the players’ friend is doubting herself before her big performance on the upcoming weekend and won’t be able to do her best, and they need to cheer her up. There’s plenty of time, and the stakes aren’t devastating, but it’s clear what needs doing, and it stokes some ideas of how to go about it.

These **sequences** are all about characters, so bring your own best roleplaying game. Don’t be afraid to spend long stretches of game time with conversations, even ones that don’t necessarily move things along.

Just because **Roleplay sequences** aren’t physically dangerous doesn’t mean it can’t be tight for your players, though. When you want to put the screws in and turn up the tension, have NPCs make bad decisions with predictable bad outcomes, stoke arguments between PCs and NPCs (or even between PCs).

Put PCs in hot water by having them **Resist Loss** when something sad happens, like after a heated argument with an NPC they care about or when a character they like makes a bad decision and something happens to them, or **Resist Doubt** when something happens to make them question their cause, like a character they look up to goes against them or tells them they’re in the wrong.

And **Roleplay sequences** don’t necessarily have guardrails. If the players can’t talk the magic heiress down before she summons a powerful demon that breaks free from her control, the **sequence** quickly becomes physically dangerous.

### Battle

:::quote
***The cursed griffin*** *that’s been prowling the cliffs late at night has finally made its first attack, placing a friend of the party in the infirmary in critical condition. The players need to get to it and either cure its curse or defeat it before it claims its next victim.****The dreamcreep from a rival House*** *has been getting ahead of himself, trapping an important person to the larger investigation into a dreamlock state. The players need to take him on and defeat him from within the dream to get them out safely and gain their favor.****Now that it’s come to light*** *that the quiet student who’s played friendly with the party is the brewshark who’s pushed poisoned brews to the party, the party needs to confront him—and it’s probably nothing short of a duel in front of the school to bring him down to size.*
:::

**Battle sequences** are dramatic, dangerous **sequences** that involve a confrontation between the players and an important force, be it a fearsome beast, a fae trickster, a demon mastermind, corrupt faculty, or another student. These **sequences** are high-stakes, thrilling, and satisfying, but will easily fatigue your players if overused, so keep them for sparing use to add to their dramatic effect.

They don’t typically just involve the battle itself, but the leadup to the confrontation too—tracking down the baddie, gathering what’s needed for the fight, getting into the location for the battle, and so on. The important part is that the stakes are high, the enemy is clear, and tension is thick throughout.

When you put your players into this **sequence,** you’re committing to stressing them out, so stick to your commitments. Make it unclear whether the players will actually make it through the fight, lead other dangerous situations up to be resolved by the dramatic battle, and make it clear that the fight is going to decide the course of the story.

This **sequence** has a star: the enemy whose battle is being built up to. Take the time to big up the baddie, make it clear how high the stakes are and how risky a battle is.

Consider whether you’re open to the players actually losing the battle. There are obviously situations where the players need to win, like a deathmatch or a final battle to resolve everything, but sometimes the story can move in some very interesting directions when the players are defeated.

You don’t need to communicate to the players that a given **Battle sequence** is set up clearly for them to win or if it’s set up with the possibility of a loss (or even that you’re assuming they’ll be defeated), but a single defeat in a **Battle sequence** like this will pretty effectively remind your players forever that brutal defeat and its painful consequences are on the table.

This **sequence** only comes out in dramatic times, so don’t pull any punches. Drop frequent Action scenes that put the players in tight spots, and push them to **Resist** all types of conditions. Build tension in other ways, too—NPCs are stressed and unhappy, people they care about are hurting, everyone is on edge and argues.

This isn’t saying to be sadistic and beat up your players to teach them a lesson. The payoff in the heavy tension of these **sequences** is when the players push through to a dramatic victory. Reward them handsomely—things going from dread and stress to being victorious and everything feeling good will have your players walking on air.

All those things you built up going wrong and feeling bad through the **sequence** can be solved with one big, dramatic confrontation that the players will remember. It’s because of this heavy weight around the accomplishment of the victory that this **sequence** isn’t one to drop at every moment.

Make sure it’s clear when the players are heading into the battle itself that this is the big moment, so they feel more comfortable burning materials and potions, pushing artifacts to breaking, and risking conditions.

After all this buildup, the last thing you want is for the battle to be anticlimactic. It shouldn’t just be a slugfest with people trading spells that force someone to **Resist.** Use a variety of threats both direct and indirect, environmental danger, and have NPCs get involved in ways that get them hurt and/or give them opportunities to shine.

The battle itself is your time on the stage, so try to be tuned into every moment once it actually happens. Keep a feel of how well things are going, and vary it—the players should get a good windfall followed by the tides turning against them, overcoming it dramatically with their own power or a sudden unexpected boon, and then tighten it again by having something go wrong, and so on.

If you have a single key enemy, consider not tracking their Resolve to determine when the battle is finished so much as deciding based on the flow. Have them react to Conditions being taken—they might take **Fear** and become irrational and lash out, or they might take **Doubt** and start just trying to raze everything down—and have the battle end in a way that feels natural once an effective moment for it arises.

It doesn’t have to end with a bad guy being struck down—for example, a rough back-and-forth battle has worn down the villain, with the group’s Mastermind instilling **Doubt,** and when the Wandjock gets them in a tight spot with a powerful spell, maybe they give up the fight, giving the players what they want, letting themselves be captured, or something like it.

### Mystery

:::quote
***The Enforcers have overlooked it****, but the party knows one of the five people in the gallery last night stole a magic item and swapped it for a fake, and they’ll have to find the culprit before they get a chance to use the magic item at the upcoming show.****Someone broke into the Eagle House halls*** *last night, smashing a display in the observatory and leaving a cryptic message that reads like a threat against one of the party members, and the party has to figure out who’s behind the act and what they’re after before they can complete whatever their strange plan is.****A professor*** *takes leave suddenly, with no warning, and everybody else seems to be acting like it’s normal, but the party alone knows how paranoid the professor had been that someone was coming for him, and he’d promised to be there to help cure a potion gone wrong. The party has to figure out what happened to him and bring him back safely before the potion incident gets worse.*
:::

**Mystery sequences** are a staple of most Starfall Academy campaigns, in all of their forms. These **sequences** involve a burning question and typically a whodunnit or howdunit aspect as the players need to get to the bottom of a situation.

These **sequences** tend to be slower-paced, more methodical, careful, and measured, and they make a great way to space something out over a longer time period. If a mystery element is set out to a week away, for example, the players will be able to practice plenty of **Downtime** activities over the week to prepare for a serious investigation on the weekend, gathering clues and building their abilities.

The work in these **sequences** is in the setup. You’ll want to be meticulous on the setup: make sure you know clearly what the mystery is and what the main elements in it are. For example, setting up a closed circle of suspects is a great way to focus a whodunnit, establishing the “scene of the crime” for a specific incident and working out for yourself the exact timeline of what happened where will help you give clues and cohesive details for the players when they investigate, and make sure you understand a good motive for why this was done.

You don’t want a **mystery sequence** to be obvious. Sometimes things might be obvious to your players and they need to work from there, find hard proof, bring somebody to justice, set something right, but those situations will be other kinds of **sequences,** like a **Roleplay** or maybe a **Dungeon.** You want your players to have to pore through the leads a little, work at it and see what shakes out.

To that end, you should make multiple answers seem believable, whether that’s an answer to “who did this,” “where did this person go,” “how did this person do this,” or anything else. In a closed-circle whodunnit situation, for example, make sure at least most people in the circle have a plausible way they might have done it, give them some secrets to hide so that they can be just as cagey as the real culprit, and give them a motive.

It often pays off to have the answer not be simple and clean. For example, in the theft of an ancient dark-magic tome, maybe one of the suspects is the one who stole it, but another one commissioned them to steal it. Maybe yet another is aiming to steal it from the one who’s commissioned it. This will allow the mystery to naturally deepen and develop as the players find clues that will often be contradictory at first.

Most of the action in these **sequences** involve questioning people, investigating scenes, tracking things down, and studying clues left behind. Be generous with clues and information: that lead you’re worried will be a smoking gun and clear it all up immediately won’t be nearly as obvious to the players, who don’t know everything and are wary of red herrings. And the players stumbling upon the truth and uncovering a mystery faster than expected is always preferable to a mystery dragging out and frustrating the players who feel like they’re never getting anything.

If you want to slow things down and confound the players, then instead of denying them clues, it can often be more effective to take the opposite route: give them lots of clues, lots of information to sift through, including false leads, irrelevant and distracting clues, and seemingly contradictory information.

A GM experienced in these **sequences** will be able to adapt the mystery based on the flow on the fly—for example, a mystery where the players take a stab in the dark with a chronomantic vision and immediately find the smoking gun can be salvaged by tossing in an extra twist, like that the person they scried swiping the artifact is being forced to do it by somebody else, or that it was actually somebody else using a glamor to look like a different suspect. Use this technique with caution—nothing kills a mystery worse than a lack of internal consistency, and if you try to pull a twist that contradicts something else you established in the mystery, you’ll back yourself into a corner trying to make it work.

These **sequences** tend to be slower and less physically dangerous than some, but you don’t want it to proceed at the same gentle, thoughtful pace all the time, or it risks becoming boring. This **sequence** lends itself well to uncovering things that might inflict **Fear,** to short **Action** scenes that involve just a few moves to keep things feeling brisk, difficult conversations that can lead to Conditions, and stumbling into traps. Don’t overuse them—they’ll have their best effect sparingly.

### Dungeon

:::quote
***The players have*** *tracked down the leads on the missing Legendary wand, but it’s being kept inside a sealed vault, and it’s anyone’s guess what’s guarding the vault inside it.****The mastermind*** *who’s been running circles around the players for weeks now has crossed the line: she’s kidnapped the party’s diviner friend, disappearing back to a griffin’s cave. The players are going to have to get in and get their friend out without riling up the griffins.****The mysterious spectral figure*** *who’s been haunting the party’s Heir hasn’t offered up any clues yet as to their true identity, but the party’s planehopper has just uncovered an extraplanar hideout that seems to belong to this mystery person—so now they just have to get in and see what they can find before the mystery person realizes their hideout has been found and vacates it.*
:::

The **Dungeon sequence** is a classic in many TTRPGs, and while it’s not as central to most of the Starfall Academy system as it is to some, it’s still an important staple of many adventures.

These **sequences** are as simple and straightforward as they are satisfying. Something that the players need is inside a dangerous place. They have to enter, solve puzzles, evade traps, and battle enemies to get through to what it is they need.

These **sequences** are a fantastic way to refresh between slower-paced **sequences** like **Mysteries** and **Roleplays,** where the players might get frustrated with a lack of clear, immediate results. These **sequences** should be enjoyable and satisfying, and give the players opportunities to use their abilities.

A **dungeon** involves rooms that the players have to make their way through, like the literal rooms of an abandoned building or the main spaces of a ley line cavern. Throw a small challenge at your players in each one, like a heavy sealed door, a trap, a chasm to cross, a creature to confront. Each one should be relatively quick to clear, lending to the brisk, satisfying pace of these **sequences,** clearing one small task after the next to get to the end.

Of course, the most dramatic, challenging, and interesting challenge should be at the end of the **dungeon,** which might be a big fight, a complex and involved trap to survive, or a dramatic and difficult escape from the **dungeon.** Reward your players with the spoils and let them enjoy being heroes making it through the dungeon—give them not only what they went in for, but plenty of materials, new spells or wands, or even a powerful new artifact.

The **dungeon** itself is the star of this **sequence,** so consider the theme and lean into it. If the players are entering a chamber full of demonic energy, don’t hold back on the creepy, haunting details, and hit the players hard with **Fear** and **Doubt.** If they’re exploring an extraplanar boundary, lean into the mind-bending energy of it with strange puzzles and risks of **Despair.** Make the **dungeon** itself memorable and distinctive and put the work into making it feel like your players are really there, and they’ll enjoy this **sequence** even more.

Or if the **dungeon** is really gross and nasty, they might enjoy it less, but, like, in the right way.

These **sequences** are often feel-good satisfying pieces of power fantasy, but they don’t have to be. You can put your players on the ropes and run them until they’re barely hanging onto their last Resolve, or even be brutal and Down them. Just keep in mind that your players will feel small and frustrated if you do, so if you do, give them an easier or more satisfying **sequence** next to make sure they don’t get helpless.

### Heist

:::quote
***The party’s friend*** *has been falsely accused and brought in for questioning at the Garrison—conveniently timed so she can’t make it to the ceremony where the villain’s plan is set to unfold. The only answer is for the party to break her out in time to save the day.****The only way*** *to stop the hex slowly draining the life away from the party’s Naturalist is with a precious potion kept in the Headmaster’s personal vault. There’s no time to brew another one—they have to steal it.****The party’s Mastermind*** *has a plan that can stop the changeling once and for all, but it’s going to need a few professors’ unwitting help. Sneaking into the professors’ offices and planting evidence to set them on the right track is the surest way to counteract the changeling’s influence and turn the tides.*
:::

The **Heist sequence** is a crucial staple of many Starfall Academy games, where the players have to break the rules and get somewhere they’re not supposed to be to achieve their objectives—and to do it without getting caught, too.

These **sequences** aren’t too dissimilar from a **Dungeon sequence,** but the **Heist** crucially involves the element of stealth and skill. Furthermore, this **sequence** is often much more than just the **Heist** itself: the preparation for the mission is just as important if not more important than the **Heist** itself. The players will often need to spend some time, including **Downtime,** gathering supplies, recruiting allies, obtaining the right potions, and learning any spells they might need, before going in for the **Heist** itself.

These **sequences** can easily run the gamut of tones just by changing the stakes. A low-stakes **Heist** will naturally lend itself to more of a whimsical **sequence**, more of a playful caper that can be good for decompressing after a stressful **sequence** or several, while a high-stakes **Heist** will be an intense thriller-like experience that will have the players on the edges of their seats. Both can be important parts of a good story, just make sure you know where on the spectrum your **Heist** falls, make sure it’s clear to the players too, and then commit to it—unless you really want to screw with your players with a dark twist, don’t start a silly, zany **Heist** and then turn it into life-or-death stakes.

That said, sometimes it can be really funny when you do.

The failure mode in these **sequences** is often not Downing as it is in other **sequences,** but in being caught. Consider carefully what that might look like for your players. Make sure you actually have a plan for what happens if they get caught and the **Heist** is busted, and not just that it’s a game-over for the players. It should be a significant setback if they get caught, not game-ending. The Trouble system is always there to help resolve this: if players are caught in the middle of a **Heist,** often the worst-case scenario is getting dragged in by the Enforcers with a bit of Trouble, which can be resolved normally, potentially even leading to new plot points, rivalries and alliances.

Leading up to the **Heist,** build an air of nervous excitement around it, and you don’t need to worry too much about the pacing, generally. But during the **Heist,** you’ll have to be on your best game for pacing. Pay careful attention to the flow, and try to make sure the party goes from boom to bust, to boom, to bust. Mix it up frequently between short scenes of high tension, short scenes of low tension, longer scenes of high tension, and longer scenes of low tension, without repeating them one after the other.

For example, maybe the party gets into a scrape with a guard and has to subdue him, in a brief, high-action scene. You might alternate it next into a long, low-action scene with figuring out a strategy to cross a room full of guards, which leads to a long, high-action scene of climbing across the ceiling and trying not to get spotted, and then into a short, high-action scene of climbing there from a window to drop and attack somebody. If things are going badly, introduce an unexpected reprieve. If things are going well, introduce an unexpected hitch.

Reward your players well for completing these. To the victors go the spoils, and you want your players to feel like victors coming out of this **sequence.**

### Don’t Listen to Any of This

Who am I to tell you what to do? Honestly.

Keep in mind that these aren’t prescriptive models telling you what your game must look like. If you follow this section exactly, with a 2- to 4-session **sequence** that fits one of these categories neatly, and then another, and then another, the campaign will quickly become rigid and uncomfortably episodic.

These are only the basic building blocks of campaign construction. In reality, every **sequence** looks more like a blend of these—a **Heist** that involves heavy **Roleplaying,** a **Mystery** centered around a **Dungeon,** and so on. And these **sequences** fit inside one another like nesting dolls: you’ll have an overall campaign along the lines of these **sequences,** like a great enemy to defeat like a **Battle sequence**, and in order to take on the bad guy the players have to find his weaknesses in an extended **Mystery sequence** that involves a **Heist** or two and a lot of **Roleplaying** sequences with his henchmen, and maybe a **Dungeon** sequence to enter his lair that involves a quick half-session **Roleplaying** sequence with a friendly creature inside the lair.

The important part is that there should always be at least one **sequence** that you can comfortably say that your players are in, and that they have an idea where it will go next. If your players are scratching their heads wondering what they’re supposed to do now, you’ll know you’ve fumbled the **sequence** structure a little bit, but that’s okay—players confuse all GMs and confound every GM’s designs at one point or another. When that does inevitably happen, come back to the drawing board, ask what the next **sequence** looks like, and have something happen that pushes them along the path towards that next objective.

And if you have a **sequence** that doesn’t feel like it fits neatly into any of these categories? That makes you an innovator. Go forth and innovate. Your players will love it.

## Action

While Starfall Academy doesn’t rely on action-packed scenes as much as some games, the **Action** system is a key part of what sets it apart from others in its field, and it merits some special attention.

**Action** is, essentially, any scenario where every second counts. There is no right or wrong time to call an **Action** scene, and any scene that could be done as an **Action** scene can also be done more narratively outside of **Action** as well, and technically speaking, any scene could be done as an **Action** scene if you really want to draw out every pulse-pounding second of the party’s Pupil reading a book.

Rather than the idea that some scenes are **Action** scenes and some aren’t, consider **Action** a tool in your toolkit to make a scene more dramatic, pump up the stakes, and count attention to every second. The same scene with an ancient pillar crumbling and falling could be a background element or a thrilling encounter, just depending on whether you decide to call it as an **Action** scene or not.

In general, **Action** is short and sweet. These aren’t turn-based encounters where people stop and think through what they want to do, so lean into what they are: brief, high-intensity, high-stakes moments that come and go as quickly as real-life action, often leaving people with heads spinning not even sure when it started or when it ended.

Of course, that’s not to say you can’t have a serious, prolonged **Action** scene. It can add a lot of weight to a given sequence if you do. Just keep in mind that a lot of gameplay mechanics are balanced around the idea that **Action** is short, so things might get a little out of whack, but you can make it work. You’re smart.

When **Action** starts, it should be clear (at least to you, the GM) who the antagonist is, even if the antagonist is the environment or a trap. **Action** is your time to shine, your stage to sing on, so everyone with performance anxiety—sorry.

You’ll often be keeping a running, breathless narration during **Action**. If the players don’t say anything at any point, then the antagonist is going to get nice, clear, open space to do whatever they want. Make frequent **Threat moves,** putting somebody or the mission at risk, and keep awarding AP to the players with each one.

Make sure your players are comfortable speaking up during **Action,** because if they don’t, they’ll never get a turn. You don’t call on people during **Action.** If they aren’t comfortable speaking up, make sure to meet them where they’re at and don’t just punish them—speak with more pauses in the narration that naturally invite people to chime in, and if people still won’t speak up with their actions, you can prod them with a call or two. “Is anybody doing anything?” or “(player’s name), do you want to do something?” shouldn’t always be necessary to get the players to take part, but can occasionally help a shy player get used to the flow of **Action.**

All that being said, don’t make it so fast that the players can’t enjoy it. When a player asks you to wait because they have something they’re thinking and just need to get it figured out, give them the space to. If the players seem overwhelmed with the pace of **Action,** slow it down. You might slow it down by just putting more pauses in the narration, or by spending more time narrating things that aren’t imminent dangers: the slow creak of a wall as it starts to collapse, the growl and the wary look of a lunar wolf as it creeps towards the players, and the slow buildup of a magic ritual as a demon emerges from a circle.

**Action** isn’t a big commitment. An **Action** scene could last for five seconds if that’s how things play out. Ending **Action** isn’t a big commitment, either, and doesn’t necessarily mean that the situation is resolved: maybe the bad guy grabs the party’s Pupil and holds her up as a human shield, and everyone pauses, the scene grinding to a tense halt as they try to talk things out. It makes sense to end **Action** there, as it’s no longer an “every second counts” situation and more a tense, high-stakes roleplaying situation, but the moment the teleporter blinks behind them and tries to wrestle the Pupil away from him, you can call **Action** again and get immediately back to it.

Overall, **Action** should be exciting and fun, and it should give players an opportunity to let loose a little. Be willing to overlook reason and rules a bit for the sake of fun in these scenes—this might be an opportunity to take that smug rival from another House who just shot off an angry spell at the players and hit him with the Shatter the party’s evoker has been waiting to use on him, without getting in trouble for it.

If your party isn’t having fun with **Action,** change up anything and everything about how it’s being done so that they are. Maybe you want to pause the action after every big **Threat move** to check in with everybody’s AP counts and see if anybody wants to do something. Maybe you want to make it feel more turn-based and call on everyone once in between **Threat moves.**

The only wrong answer is to continue doing something your players aren’t enjoying—unless that “something your players aren’t enjoying” is a bloody thrasher beating them to a pulp when they trespassed on its territory, because, you know? Sometimes the players deserve it.

## Rewards

The only thing better than making progress is making progress and getting good stuff at the same time.

As the GM, you have a variety of rewards at your fingertips to dish out anytime the players have earned it (or maybe just when you want to make them feel good). These range from game-changing to little “oh, neat” microdoses of dopamine, so you can deliver them based on the situation.

The clearest moment to give rewards, aside from anything game-mandated like **Downtime** or from attending classes, is when the players have accomplished something big.

When they get to the end of a **Dungeon,** they root around and find not only the secret scroll they’re looking for, but also 800 materials and the potion recipe for **De La Montagne** as well, for example. When they solve a mystery and prove a character guilty of the crime the party had been framed for, the Enforcers not only clear the players’ names but also let them have the mysterious artifact confiscated from the criminal. And so on.

These help your players grow and progress as they accomplish things, and it makes the feeling of success and achievement on the other side of a big win more concrete.

That said, especially with smaller rewards like small amounts of materials, a potion, or a glyph, you can give them out at any moment. It can make for a great all-purpose good thing to have happen, which can be helpful in lots of scenarios. For example, a character is digging around into a complete nonsense lead that won’t get them anywhere, but they make a stellar roll to investigate. Instead of taking the wind out of a great roll result with “there’s nothing,” you can turn it around with, “turns out this is a dead lead—nothing down this way. The investigation doesn’t end up for nothing, though, as you can scrounge 150 materials from the abandoned room.”

Potions are especially nice as a small reward to hand out, especially if your players aren’t taking a lot of potions. Players can often tend towards a habit of conserving single-use items forever, so making sure they regularly stumble upon more potions helps ease that feeling, and you can also pick potions that you know will be helpful for upcoming challenges. For example, if you know the party is about to get into a situation where they need to escape a crumbling building, and the party’s Enforcer searches a room for anything good, you can have them happen upon a **Dragonfly** potion that might just save their butts when things get dangerous.

It should go without saying, but make sure you only give useful rewards. If nobody in your party has any ranks in **Alchemy,** giving them a high-level potion recipe is only going to mock them. The learning materials for an Advanced spell in a subject nobody has any ranks in are going to collect dust. That doesn’t mean you can’t branch out—giving a simple potion recipe to a group without an alchemist might get somebody to pick up a little bit of potion-brewing, and the learning materials for an easy Standard spell in a new subject might help a character in the group branch out a little and start exploring some new magic. Just consider what’s really most helpful.

Of course, maybe check yourself before you go giving rewards that are far too useful. Handing out a 10,000-material wand to a first-year in the first three sessions is going to make everybody else in the party feel useless. Having the party check under someone’s bed and find 50,000 materials is going to imbalance the rest of the game. And giving too many glyphs or recipes or spell-learning materials will just overwhelm your players.

There’s an art to finding the right balance. When in doubt, go for more, smaller rewards. Nothing like a steady drip of dopamine.

In general, 100 materials per person is a small but noticeable amount to give, 500 materials is good, and 1000 is a huge boon. Of course, where exactly the numbers fall will depend a lot on your party—a bunch of first-year spellslingers will go through materials so slowly that 100 will feel like a decent number, while a fourth-year alchemist or trader will go through so many that 500 might look like a joke. Just try to get a feel for your party, and remember it’s not the end of the world if you give too many or too few.

Artifacts, though, can be another story altogether. These are the rarest and most powerful rewards to give out, and they can change the flow of a game altogether, especially for higher-level ones. That’s not to say you shouldn’t give them in a game, especially if your party has an Artificer, but it does mean that these are the juicy, sparkling spoils that get everybody excited.

## Conditions and Downing

Sometimes the players have a beatdown coming for them.

How exactly you call for Resists and handle Conditions can make your game feel stylistically very different from another, or help you adjust flow within the same game. Although there are things that automatically force the players to Resist, such as a spell backfire, it shouldn’t be the only time the players run the risk of racking up Conditions unless it’s deliberately a very low-stakes campaign.

### Resist Difficulty

In general, more Resist rolls mean higher tension. If you have more frequent low-DC Resist rolls, it creates a sense of constant attrition and that the PCs are being worn down and won’t be able to go on forever, while if you have less frequent high-DC Resist rolls, it creates a tense atmosphere with everybody on edge wondering when the next threat is going to hit them. Apply either one as necessary to build tension in tight scenes.

Typically, Resist rolls are a consequence for failing another roll, providing a two-roll buffer between a PC and a potential Condition, but if you really want to dial up the tension, you can jump straight to the Resist roll. For example, the eyes of a twelve-toothed gloomcatcher opening from across the room and staring into the Alchemist’s eyes could go three ways:

1. Terrifying flavor text: the Alchemist’s heart pounds, and they draw their wand staggering back from it.
2. Danger is coming: the Alchemist must roll Willpower against a set DC or be forced to Resist Fear.
3. Five-alarm panic: the Alchemist must Resist Fear.

Jumping straight to the Resist roll is a great way to signal to the players that the threat might be more than they can handle. That said, unless you’re trying for a dark, gritty campaign, you should avoid overusing it—Conditions are hard to clear, and every Resist roll has at least a one-in-five failure chance, so it could easily rack up dangerously high if you keep using it.

### When to Resist What

It’s easy to fall into a rhythm of letting spell backfires set the pace of Resist rolls and only call extra Resist rolls when someone is getting hurt, but the Condition system is meant to represent mental strain as well as physical strain. You can increase the tension of any scene by calling for appropriate Resist rolls.

For example, an argument with a close friend could change from just being sad to leaving painful repercussions for the party if a PC has to Resist Loss for the emotional impact of feeling like they’re losing a friend. A smooth-talking mastermind whispering influence into a PC’s ear could change from smug monologuing to an insidious threat if the PC has to Resist Doubt, and a reveal that the artifact the heist was supposed to retrieve is already long gone when the PCs get there could shatter the PCs’ resolve by forcing them to Resist Despair.

For some examples of when different Resist rolls can be appropriate, check out the following table:

| **CONDITION** | **CONTEXT** | **EXAMPLES** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FEAR** | **IMMINENT DANGER** | - A terrifying creature emerges&#10;  - A villain reveals a terrible power&#10;  - The party is surrounded by Enforcers mid-heist&#10;  - A magic phenomenon erupts explosively and dangerously&#10;  - The party loses their escape route or trump card |
| **DESPAIR** | **HOPE IS LOST, NO USE IN TRYING** | - An extraplanar effect poisons a PC’s mind&#10;  - The party’s objective is suddenly out of reach&#10;  - A villain tries to convince the PC their cause is hopeless&#10;  - The PCs keep failing at a task&#10;  - A great effort turns out to have been for nothing |
| **WOUND** | **PHYSICAL PAIN, SICKNESS** | - A PC is hit by some dangerous effect&#10;  - The PC is poisoned&#10;  - A lingering spell effect leaves a PC suffering&#10;  - Volatile magic reacts inside the PC’s body&#10;  - Being around hexological magic takes its toll |
| **LOSS** | **EMOTIONAL PAIN, SORROW** | - A social interaction goes devastatingly badly&#10;  - The PC is rejected in a way that’s meaningful to them&#10;  - Something terrible happens to a loved one&#10;  - The PC fails in a way that makes them feel inadequate&#10;  - Somebody plays to the PC’s insecurities |
| **DOUBT** | **QUESTIONING LOYALTIES** | - A villain tries to convince the PC they’re on the wrong side&#10;  - The PC sees terrible consequences of their own actions&#10;  - A demon whispers darkness into the PC’s mind&#10;  - Somebody tempts the PC with influence and power&#10;  - Hexological magic exerts its temptation on a PC |

It’s important, with these and calls of what to Resist for a spell backfire, to maintain some variety and not just rely on one, but it can help build a specific type of tension to lean more on calls for one specific Condition.

For example, a morally gray sequence or one dealing with demons will call for more Resist Doubt rolls, and an emotionally loaded roleplay sequence with difficult feelings and relationships fraying will call for Resist Loss rolls.

When in doubt, Resist Wound is there to be a general fallback: part of the use of Body as a stat is that Wound tends to be the most common Condition, and many magic effects are set up such that Wound is easier to clear than other Conditions.

### I’ve Got Conditions

The Condition system doesn’t begin and end with a Resist roll. What you actually do once your players have taken Conditions can change the tone.

By default, the mechanics don’t change anything from having more Conditions, but if you want to drive in the sensation of being battered, you can impose penalties for Conditions. A character with three stacks of Fear will likely have a much higher DC for a Willpower check to stand up against the necrotic dragon, while a character suffering from Doubt will have a higher DC for acts of leadership like delivering a rousing speech to their team.

You can encourage your players to roleplay their Conditions as well, and to consider what their characters might do based on their Conditions. A PC changing their approach in a battle scene when they’ve suffered multiple stacks of Wound to be more defensive and careful might be sensible, realistic play to reward with lower DCs or even an improvement roll for a defensive and cautious Ability like Endurance or Warding if used appropriately.

And of course, sometimes, a PC will hit the much-feared 0 Resolve. Because of how often games treat analogous states as a flat-out binary failure, players will often feel frustrated or upset getting to 0 Resolve, so encourage it as a roleplaying opportunity and work out what defeat looks like for their character. Try to find a way that a partial defeat or even the whole party being Downed might lead to the plot advancing or new leads arising, and since being Downed is a painful and traumatic experience for the character, work with the player to try to decide how it’s taken its toll on their character. Maybe they have a new fear, a new trauma, or maybe it’s given them a hardened resolve or a new goal, to get revenge, to protect it from happening to other people, to make amends, or similar.

Defeat is often a learning experience, so it can make sense to give improvement rolls to Downed characters once the situation is settled, as well! Everybody loves a comeback story where the hero learns from sparking out and comes back stronger.

Conditions take time to clear. Consider taking extended periods of downtime if multiple party members are at very low Resolve, if it makes sense for the story and the timeline.

You can also clear a Condition any time you like, but try to use it sparingly so that the players don’t start counting on divine intervention. Make sure it’s something that makes sense: for example, the mastermind planting seeds of doubt into the ear of the party’s Rascal might inflict Doubt, but when the Rascal finds out later that the mastermind was lying, it would make sense to clear that stack of Doubt, maybe even with an improvement roll in Read Person to reflect their learning experience.

### Conditions for NPCs

Technically speaking, Conditions and Resolve work the same way for NPCs as they do for PCs, and a typical human NPC will have 5 Resolve just like the PCs.

However, generally, it’s better for the flow to treat NPCs as if they have only one or two Resolve. The party’s Mastermind successfully seeding Doubt into the mind of the Enforcer guard might turn them right away, instead of needing to inflict an arbitrary further 4 Condition stacks to Down them, and successfully inflicting Wound on another student might knock them out right away. Tougher NPCs might have 2 Resolve: the rogue arcanist might be able to take a Wound and keep going, but then inflicting a stack of Fear finally breaks them, and they run.

Similarly, creatures have Resolve scores listed, but that’s a default number, and you can always adjust it to suit the flow. A **steelnose,** for example, has extremely high Resolve owing to them being difficult to kill, but they are stated to be very cowardly, so inflicting Wounds might do little to nothing, but a single stack of Fear might Down them at your discretion.

Or maybe someone inflicted a stack of Wound on a **bloody thrasher,** but they did it with 6 degrees of success and a roll of 44, so you might want to skip all the numbers and just say the **bloody thrasher** is bloodied and thrashed. I mean, at that point, they’ve probably earned it.

## Abilities and When to Call Them

Along with their selection of spells, a PC’s choices of **Ability** score rankings is the biggest determinator of how the character plays mechanically. But of course, if you never call **Ability** rolls, it kind of goes to waste.

As a reminder, **Abilities** is an umbrella category that encompasses both **Skills** and **Subjects,** such as Recall Information, Investigation, Persuasion, Illusion, or Necromancy. The core flow of gameplay hinges around them: the GM describes the scenario, the players describe what they want to do, and the GM adjudicates the results, having them roll their **Abilities** if necessary to determine how it goes.

**Subjects** aren’t typically rolled directly, usually rolled by casting spells, but it is appropriate at times, typically when identifying a given magic phenomenon, item or creature associated with the given field. **Skill** rolls, on the other hand, are common, while rolling a **Stat** directly (e.g. a Logic roll) is very unusual and only associated with specific game effects such as **Resist** rolls.

The most important thing, though, is to **seek variety in the rolls you call for.** That’s not to say you shouldn’t focus more on one for a given circumstance: it makes sense that a given sequence might lean more towards one set of **Abilities** than another.

For example, climbing an untrodden, wild mountain path will require a lot of **Athletics** and **Agility** rolls, while sneaking through an archive to try finding secret information will require a lot of **Search** and **Research** and **Stealth** rolls, while racing a wyvern through Dolphin House might require some **Comprehend** rolls to try to figure out what made you think that was a good idea in the first place.

But varying the challenges to try to target a wider variety of **Abilities** is generally a good tool to help your players leverage their strengths—and have their weaknesses be their downfall. The downfall, of course, is the best part.

If you just finished a scene that called for a lot of **Body** checks, try to move to one that involves more **Charm** checks, or maybe stealth-based checks like **Hide Object, Sleight of Hand,** and **Stealth.**

Of course, calling an **Ability** roll **doesn’t necessarily have to represent high stakes or challenge.** Because of the degrees-of-success system that Starfall Academy uses, calling a check can be a way for a character to achieve even higher than regular success.

A player asking to search the room for the artifact shard they’re after and automatically succeeding is one thing, but being able to roll a **Search** roll and getting 3 degrees of success might lead to them getting even more than they were after—maybe a plot hook you wanted to find a place to work in, or a potion that’s going to be helpful for an upcoming encounter, or if you don’t have any ideas, just giving out some materials can be a good bonus too.

### DCs and Degrees

Deciding what to call for a given DC can be an art form in and of itself. When in doubt, consider that **the average roll result for a roll with no bonuses or modifiers is 11, and add or subtract 5 for each degree of difficulty.** Something that the average, uninitiated arcanist could pull off half the time would be a DC11, while DC16 represents something with a degree of challenge that is reasonably doable for someone with some experience in the field and is possible but unlikely for somebody uninitiated, and a DC21 is impossible for the uninitiated to pull off and requires some extensive expertise to pull it off with any degree of regularity.

Meanwhile, **DC5 is commonly used for rolls that don’t have any real risk of failure, to see how someone performs when failure is not a consideration**. For example, a DC5 **Recall Information** roll to remember a conversation a PC had would be a question of how much the PC remembers exactly from it, rather than a test to see if they remember or not. One who only rolls 1 degree of success would remember only the surface-level topics of the conversation, while one who rolls 6 degrees of success might remember entire sections of it word-for-word.

It is not actually impossible to fail these rolls. In general, when someone does, it is hilarious.

Except in higher-level campaigns, DCs above 30 are unlikely to have a player reach them unless they’re burning higher-level abilities like **Golden Wellspring** and **Wandjock: All In.**

But that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t call them! Just like how an easy check can represent the degree to which a character succeeds, an impossible roll can be useful to represent the degree to which a character fails. If the party’s Enforcer tries to grapple with a greater metal golem and block it from passing through, having them make a **DC33 Endurance** roll can tell you how it goes. One degree of failure might be “a valiant effort at the impossible” and look like the Enforcer holding the golem back long enough to buy the party some time before getting pushed aside, while five degrees of failure could be a catastrophic failure that would likely result in the Enforcer having to **Resist Wound.** As well as, potentially, whoever gets hit by the Enforcer when they’re sent flying.

The degrees of success system gives you the opportunity to cover a whole spread of outcomes, and it lets players lean into their strengths. Although it might be tempting to adapt a DC to the player, such as setting a higher **Persuasion** DC to someone who you know has a +12 total **Persuasion** roll, it would defeat the purpose of PCs building strong **Ability** ranks.

Try to keep an open mind to all different possibilities: instead of ruling something as impossible, call it at an appropriately high DC, so that characters who are capable of incredible feats are able to pull it off. If the party’s Rascal actually rolls that 44 to do a backflip into the open window of a passing car, at that point, they’ve earned it.

Higher degrees of success can be a challenge to adjudicate. Spells often have higher-level behavior baked in, but other checks don’t necessarily have guides.

A character getting high degrees of success on a **Recall Information** roll to try to remember what they’ve heard about a given character before might learn more advanced information, even secrets, or for a very good roll, you might even ask the player to weigh in on a piece of information they’ve heard, allowing them to influence the story a little by deciding something about the target.

Or it could be called as that higher degrees of success mean there’s a personal connection. Maybe a dramatic 4-degree-of-success roll prompts a flashback sequence where the characters interact in a way that gives some critical information to the present situation.

There’s no right or wrong way to adjudicate higher degrees of success and failure (okay, there are some wrong ways, but I trust you know better). When in doubt, ask the player to weigh in, too, on what they want—maybe they want their character to pull off their attempt with so much flair it impresses someone, or maybe they want to obtain a cool trinket or some materials in the process, or maybe they just want a superhero-esque description of how epically they pull off their badass maneuver.

Of course, an improvement roll is always in your court to call for. Just don’t fall into the cycle of giving more and more improvements in the same **Ability** for rolling well in that **Ability.**

## Establishing Expectations

It’s helpful, when you run a game, to make sure that everyone is playing the same game. When you start running a game, you’ll want to check in with your players about what kind of game, exactly, everyone is looking for.

In many TTRPG circles, this is typically referred to as **session zero,** discussing with your players before starting the game, to make sure that everyone is on the same page with what they want and don’t want. For example, if your players are looking for a lighthearted cozy mystery and you spring high-stakes chase scenes and character deaths on them, your players will be disappointed and you’ll be disappointed that people are reacting to your cool game with horror instead.

In this kind of check-in, you’ll want to take stock of anything your players want to see—what kind of story tropes and themes they want to explore, as well as any they want to avoid.

For example, do your players want to deal with a morally ambiguous villain with complex, sprawling schemes, in a dark storyline dealing with the weight and responsibility of secrets? Or do they want a power fantasy where they get to stand up firmly for what’s right against a misguided or outright wicked villain? Or do they want a story without a villain at all, a low-stakes improvisational game that focuses on relationships between characters?

Try to figure out what each player wants for their character as well. Maybe one player wants his character to become a great spellslinger and contender for the House Championships, while another player wants her character to overcome her personal insecurities and face down her personal villain, while another player just wants their character to learn as many spells as possible. These kinds of requests aren’t exactly binding agreements—you’re not obligated to overturn a story to give a character every detail of the story they’re after, and a player might change the course they want for their character over the course of a story, so there’s no need to be married to what your players say on this point, but it can help give your players more ownership of the game and story.

When you do try to scope out what your players want for their characters, try to figure out both an **external arc** and an **internal arc.**

| **EXTERNAL ARC** | **INTERNAL ARC** |
| --- | --- |
| **Experiences to go through,** challenges posed by the world around the character | **Emotional challenges to process,** personal growth and the experiences to provoke it |
| **EXAMPLES** |   |
| “I want my character to tame a dragon.”“I’d like to see my character get into dueling culture and win a lot of duel sigils.”“I want to solve a big, ancient mystery.”“My character wants to uncover forgotten powers.”“My character’s goal is to wield necromancy and commune with a distant relative.”“My character wants to discover the secrets of their unstable magic power.” | “My character is closed-off and I want to see her learn to trust.”“I want my character to start off really naive but grow more mature.”“I’d like to see my character tempted by power so she has to weigh her loyalties with her desire for power.”“My character is greedy and wants all the materials they can get their hands on, and I want them to grow past that.”“I want my character to face her fear of betrayal.”“My character is prone to anger, and she’ll probably be burned and grow from it.” |

Your players may not all provide all of this at **session zero**. That’s okay! Maybe a given player only has an idea of an external arc and they want to play their character to get an idea of their internal arc, or maybe they only have an internal arc and want you to take the lead on guiding them towards an external arc. If they’re not sure, talk it through with them and help them figure out if there’s one that suits their character.

Things arising in the story will likely change both your and your players’ perceptions of the characters. For example, encountering two factions in a rivalry with one another might make one of your players take up a new external arc of trying to smooth out the tensions between the two factions and make another of your players take up a new internal arc of being aligned with one faction while dealing with feelings for someone aligned with the other side. Encourage and lean into these changes! A responsive, reactive story helps bring out the best in the GM’s world and in the players’ characters, too.

Keep communications open with your players frequently to check in with what they’re looking for in the story. This helps your players feel heard, but it also helps make sure your story, as the GM, is being appreciated and enjoyed to the maximum, and will often help give you some inspirations about where to take your story.

### Romance

Romances are a common part of many TTRPGs, and Starfall Academy especially leans into it. This is the favorite part of many players, but is also something to approach carefully. This is one of the most important places to figure out the boundaries and expectations, so work it out cautiously and you’ll have some great rewards on the other side.

#### NPC-NPC

When it comes to NPC-NPC romances, you’re safe to proceed as you like—nothing fraught in this category except for how awkward it can be, as the GM, to sit there and flirt with yourself out loud in front of an audience.

#### PC-NPC

For PC-NPC romances, it’s important to discuss with your players before the game and figure out what they want. A given player might be fully open to their character having a romantic arc, or might want to complete an internal arc first or over the course of a romantic arc—for example, “my character is afraid of letting people in close, so she’ll have to get past that first or find someone who breaks down her walls.”

Either way, never push or force a romance when the player isn’t feeling it. An NPC might flirt with a PC, but respect the player’s decision if they don’t want to pursue it. (There’s some real-life advice in there, too, for any of you not sure how to handle your real-life romances.)

The subject of PCs’ romances is something players will often want to revisit later on. Check in after a few sessions once the PCs’ dynamics with the NPCs have been more established, and figure out if they’re looking for a relationship dynamic for their character or not.

A player might also have no interest in romance for their character. This is the easy outcome to handle: don’t give them a romance. That said, give them space if they want to change their mind, but they should be the one to come to you about it if they do, just make sure they know the door is open to tell you if they do. (There’s some real-life advice in there too.)

It should go without saying that you should not use a PC-NPC romance as a pretense to actually flirt with the player. Check in with everyone’s boundaries and make sure everyone’s comfortable. And if you want to flirt with a player, I’m not saying you can’t, but find the right time and place for it.

#### PC-PC

For PC-PC romances, this one is more out of your control, but is also a sensitive subject. Unless the two players are together, you’ll want to check in with this one and make sure both sides are respecting each other’s boundaries and not awkwardly blurring any lines. (And if the two players are together, you’ll want to check in and make sure they’re not making out at the table or anything. Unless the rest of the table is into that. I’m not gonna cramp your style.)

PC-PC romances are easier to figure out from session zero, since most players will have clear ideas if they’re open to one or not and about their boundaries around them. If it looks like a PC-PC romance is arising, just keep an eye on it and make sure to check in, ideally privately, with each person involved to make sure they’re comfortable.

If both parties are enthusiastically participating, then by all means, let them go for it. This is where you get to wield your power and make it just so happen that the hideout everyone is laying low in for the night has only one bed for the two of them. What a shame! What do you suppose they’ll do?

### Game Style

TTRPGs are, at their core, improvisational theater. Starfall is no exception, just with magic and gay people. Actually, wait, that’s just regular improvisational theater.

Another thing to work out beforehand with your players is what level of improvisation you and your players want in a game. Some games involve the GM primarily running the show while the players just manage their own parts within the scene, while some games put much more storytelling power in the hands of the players. There’s no right or wrong way to do it, just a matter of which one you and your players want to pursue.

#### Guided

In a **guided** game style, you as the GM control the stage and set the scene. If you’re playing a **guided** game, you have a clear idea what the storyline will be, what the important settings will be, who the relevant characters will be, and what the players should, generally, be doing at any point.

You’ll likely have a clear sense of logical flow from one plot point to the next. For example, take the following GM’s plan for a **guided** game:

:::quote
**SESSION PLAN****The current scene:** Carlos, Maya, and Finn are infiltrating a private event to grab a stolen artifact before it can be delivered to its buyer.**Complications:** The players will find out that Maya’s friend Eliza is helping run the event. The artifact is a fake, and the real stolen artifact is being kept in a more secure location in the venue. The event has two black-clawed walkers that guard it as security, especially the item exhibition.**Characters’ status:** Maya just had an argument and is frustrated trying to prove herself. Carlos is still rocking a lot of conditions and will stay out of the action, mostly. Maya is on duty keeping a straight face and playing the part pretending to be an honored guest at the event. Carlos and Finn will probably be staying quiet, letting Maya do the talking.They’ll end up in a conversation with Stephens, who will try to obfuscate and subtly test them because he suspects a mole somewhere in this event. The conversation will end up getting strained, but if they lose credibility too much, they’re saved by the item exhibition starting up. Maya will try to get into the exhibition itself and find Eliza running it—cue dramatic reveal. Eliza recognizes her as an old friend and welcomes her, and Maya has to temper her feelings. Finn can use some subtle telekinesis to swipe the stolen artifact, but it becomes clear it’s a fake. Carlos can make a distraction for Finn and Maya to get into the back, where they have to get past a golem guarding a chest and Stephens, who’s tracked them here. Maya’s new summoning technique will help turn the golem against Stephens and get them through the encounter.They’ll be able to swipe 600 materials from the back and the **Shadow Sneak** potion recipe.Once they grab the real stolen artifact, Enforcer Marisa crashes the event, and the party can sneak out in the chaos.
:::

In this style of game, the players are directed into scenes, but then have more freedom within those scenes to solve predefined problems laid out by the GM. In a **guided** game, the GM has full control over everything that isn’t the PCs’ direct actions.

This style of game gives the GM finer control and allows a rich, cohesive story to unfold, but some GMs dislike it for its sense of putting everything on the GM to figure out and some players dislike it for its feeling of being “on rails,” like they don’t really get to influence the story itself and are just playing their parts.

Here’s an example of **guided** play, within that same scene:

:::dialog
**Helena (GM):** Maya leads the way across the polished ballroom floor, symphonic music filling the space under its glimmering chandeliers and contrasting gratingly with the scraping sound of black-clawed walkers on the hardwood. Finn and Carlos stick close behind, and you spot the tall, imposing figure of **Professor Richard Stephens** here with an effortlessly polished look, a suit vest with pressed trousers, dark eyes gleaming as he talks to a younger artificer.
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Do we know the younger artificer?

**Ava (Finn):** Maybe don’t tell him that.

Seems like he’s trying to be better than his faculty colleagues by getting into sketchy things like this event. He’s being cool and aloof because he thinks it will endear the powerful at this underground event.

**Helena (GM):** Yeah, not bad at all. Roll **Deception,** and since you’ve gotten such a good read on him and are needling his insecurities, it’ll be easy, a **DC12** check.
:::

#### Improvisational

In an **improvisational** game style, the GM still runs the show and is the last word on everything, but the players have more creative input on what’s happening beyond just their own actions. If you’re running an **improvisational** game, you will likely have a clear idea of the setup—problems that the players must resolve, obstacles to resolving them, and allies who want to help them get there, stakes to make those problems real, potential rewards for resolving them—but a looser framework on how they get there.

In an **improvisational** game, it’s up to the players to figure out how to solve problems, but the story is also theirs to tell, with the GM as the final arbiter. Players may ask to introduce something to the story: for example, when encountering a fearsome beast, maybe the group’s Naturalist speaks up and says her character has had experiences with these beasts before, but also some lingering trauma and fear around those experiences.

As the GM, you might choose to allow it, to deepen the character’s personal attachment to the game and the story, or you might choose to veto it if it contradicts the game you have in mind, although it might be done in a way that still gives the player something close to what they ask for—in the Naturalist example, you might say something like, “You’ve worked with countless close relatives to this beast, everything in its family, but this one, the apex predator, you’ve known to stay away from,” to give the character some more of the personal connection desired while still keeping the story in line with your intention.

You might also choose the “yes, and” approach of giving them what they ask for, along with a twist. If the Naturalist says that she’s encountered this type of beast before, you might say, “You’ve worked with these creatures before, and you know from looking at it that there’s something deeply wrong with this one—the way it moves, the glassy-eyed stare, there’s something missing from its essence in a way that gives you chills.”

Here’s an example of **improvisational** gameplay:

:::dialog
**Helena (GM):** Maya leads the way across the polished ballroom floor, symphonic music filling the space under its glimmering chandeliers and contrasting gratingly with the scraping sound of black-clawed walkers on the hardwood. Finn and Carlos stick close behind, and you spot the tall, imposing figure of **Professor Richard Stephens** here with an effortlessly polished look, a suit vest with pressed trousers, dark eyes gleaming as he talks to a younger artificer.
**Kayla (Maya):** Oh, can we say I know the artificer he’s talking to? Maya’s probably been sniffing around the people connected to this event.
**Helena (GM):** Yeah, I can see that. This is **Emelia Hawkins,** first-year artificer. Give me a **Read Person** roll to see how much you’ve dug up on her beforehand, **DC11**.
**Kayla (Maya):** (rolls) Twenty, two degrees of success. What do I get?
**Helena (GM):** You pinpointed some details of her before—she’s a close associate of the White Tiger ring, although she doesn’t have any magical legacy behind her. Her family is wealthy in mundane fields, but she’s the first to really pursue magic, so your assumption is probably that she’s bought her way in with flatmats. You were able to dig up that she’s insecure about her position here and posturing highly to try to impress people.
**Ava (Finn):** If she’s been buying her way into places and trying to posture, maybe we can say Finn knows her? Or vice-versa?
**Helena (GM):** I don’t think she and Finn run in the same circles, but… maybe we can say you both have a mutual contact and you know *of* each other. In fact—why don’t we say this? You know she’s heard some nasty things about you and probably thinks you’re a monster.
**Ava (Finn):** I love it. I think Finn’s going to stand at the sidelines and wait to come in as the bad guy, then.
**Helena (GM):** You can absolutely creep off to the side somewhere to blend in. So you’re going to approach Emelia, ignoring the professor for now?
**Kayla (Maya):** Yeah, I think maybe we can get through to the professor via her, if they were chatting. I’ll go up to her and flatter her, like, “Oh, Miss Hawkins, isn’t it? I saw your demonstration at the Midnight Exhibition the other night, I didn’t think I’d meet you here.”(Helena checks her notes—she’d originally been planning that nobody had been at the Midnight Exhibition and that something happened there that would come up later, but she decides it’s more fun this way, and she adjusts some things, settling in to roleplay Emelia and reference the thing that happened at the Midnight Exhibition as foreshadowing for later.)
:::

#### Freeform

In a **freeform** game style, the GM and the players come up with the story together, the GM more serving as the main guide for the story rather than the arbiter of decisions and storytelling. If you’re running a **freeform** game, you may very well know nothing about where the story is going and what the characters are going to get into. Be prepared to think on your feet.

In a **freeform** game, the line between player and GM is blurred, as everybody has some say over the story. You can still veto anything—as the GM, you remain the final arbiter of the game—but your role is less to use that to create a story you have in mind and more to maintain consistency, make sure everybody’s having a good time, and that boundaries are respected.

For instance, maybe you’ve introduced a villain, and one of the players decides that this villain is her secret paramour. That might wildly disrupt the plans you’ve had and introduce new dynamics you hadn’t considered, and if it’s too much then you could still veto it, but in general in this game style, you’ll roll with it.

However, you should especially be aware of any conflicts, whether with other things in the game or with the other players’ wants. When you do veto something, though, try to go with the “no, but” style of giving them something similar that might still fulfill their wants.

For example, maybe the villain is much older and that dynamic is something another player doesn’t want to see, so you might cross it out with, “That would be an uncomfortable age gap issue to explore, so I’m going to cross that out, but maybe your secret paramour is her daughter instead.”

Maybe the player’s declaration interrupts continuity—for example, the character has sworn before that she’d never get entangled with that family, in which case you might challenge it with, “That would go against your oath as stated before, so maybe we can say that you’ve had secret late-night rendezvous that have tempted you to break your vows, instead?”

Or it could just be that it won’t be any fun, especially if it takes away from another player. If another player is supposed to have a deep, loaded connection with this villain and the “secret paramour” declaration gets in the way of that, then you might cross it out to give that other player their moment, maybe with something like, “That’s going to conflict with this other character’s personal story, so I’d prefer to avoid that. Maybe we can say you know this woman because your secret paramour is her close partner in crime instead?”

That said, in general in a **freeform** game style, you’ll be looking to say yes to as much as you can, even if you have to change things. Maybe you never mentioned the villain’s age, so if it’s a wide age gap that makes another player uncomfortable, you can make her younger so it fits. If it contradicts that character’s sworn vow, you could make the villain a renegade from her family so that it isn’t really breaking the vow. If it takes away from another player’s big moment, you could introduce another villain, one to be the secret paramour and one to give the other character a big moment.

Remember that the story in a **freeform** game is negotiable, and that means you get to negotiate too. If the players put you in a position where you don’t know how to resolve it, you can ask them for ideas on where it should lead. For example, if a player decides there’s a powerful artifact hidden somewhere in the building that could stop the villains from completing their scheme, and you’re not sure how to work that in, you should feel empowered to say, “That sort of trivializes the moment you’ve worked towards here, though—do you think maybe there’s more to it than that?” and work out with the player that maybe the artifact is attuned to somebody else and they have to get their hands on it and try bluffing their way through the situation, pretending they can use the artifact and thus intimidating the villains away.

#### Your Game

Nothing is ever so cut and dry, and your game will probably fall somewhere in between these three. Think of it more as a spectrum from highly **guided** to totally **freeform**, with **improvisational** games being in the middle, and you can move along this spectrum as you like as well.

For example, your game might be highly **guided** while the players are infiltrating a secret hideout, but then be more **freeform** while in a low-stakes roleplaying scene later on. Or you may start the game with a very **guided** style to help the players acclimate to their characters and the setup and then move towards an **improvisational** style once they’ve gotten comfortable, or start a **freeform** game that becomes more **guided** as a cohesive plot naturally begins to emerge.

Ultimately, the decision just comes down to you and your players. This is another topic to scope out in your **session zero** and then check back in on as the game progresses—if players are feeling too aimless, you might need a more **guided** structure, and if they’re feeling too stifled, you might need a more **freeform** structure.

Ultimately, the only rule is to respect each other’s boundaries. Wherever it is that the story takes you and your players, go boldly.

And as for all the rules that do exist at Starfall, well, rules are made to be broken.
