## Rolling and DCs (Difficulty Classes)

The gameplay in the Starfall Academy system works like any typical TTRPG: the GM describes the situation, you say what you want your character to do, and the GM resolves what happens as a result, possibly asking you to roll a check to determine if you’re successful.

If the GM does require you to roll, the die roll in question is 2d10, or two ten-sided dice, against a number called the **Difficulty Class,** or **DC,** which represents the challenge of the task. For example, a task that’s only **DC5** is very easy to complete, whereas a **DC30** task is extremely challenging and requires a high level of mastery.

If you do need to roll to complete a task, the GM will tell you what to roll and what DC to roll against, and you will roll 2d10 and add the relevant modifiers and compare it to the DC to see how many **degrees of success** (or **degrees of failure**) you get.

Degrees of success (or failure) represent how much you succeeded (or failed) by. For example, rolling a 25 on a DC10 check is a bigger success than rolling a 12, while rolling a 3 on a DC20 check is a bigger failure than rolling an 18.

You get 1 degree of success for beating or tying the DC, and for every 5 over you are, you get 1 more degree of success. Similarly, you get 1 degree of failure for being below the DC, and for every 5 under you are, you get 1 more degree of failure.

That might sound a bit confusing. Here’s an example dialog:

:::dialog
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I want to study the runes, see if I can work out some kind of pattern.
**Helena (GM):** Sure. You look closer at the runes, which seem to link together somehow, but you can’t quite make it out… roll **Analyze, DC14.**
**Ahmed (Carlos):** You got it.(Ahmed rolls 2d10 and gets a 2 and a 4, really just embarrassing himself outright. He adds his character Carlos’s Logic, 0, and Analyze rank, 0, for a 6.)
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Well… it’s a 6.(Helena counts out—6 is below the DC, so it’s a failure. It’s below the DC by more than 5, so it’s a second degree of failure, but not by more than 10, so it’s only two degrees of failure.)
**Helena (GM):** So, that’s two degrees of failure, which means you have absolutely no idea what you’re looking at. Furthermore—trying to fuss with the runes, you’ve awakened something in them, and a toxic power leaks out, a noxious energy crackling in the air around you—
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I run away.
**Helena (GM):** Smart move. Roll **Athletics, DC10.**(Ahmed rolls 2d10 and gets a 9 and a 10. He adds Carlos’s Body, 5, and Athletics rank, 5, for a 29.)
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I got a 29!(Helena counts the degrees of success—29 is above the DC, which gives a degree of success, and beats it by more than 15, for 3 additional degrees of success, for a grand total of 4 degrees of success. Helena now has to come up with something to represent that level of success.)
**Helena (GM):** Wow, okay. You run faster than anyone’s ever run before. In fact—I’ll let you drag your entire party along with you, getting all of you away from danger, up to where—well, normally you’d have to leap over the chasm, but I think Carlos just throws everyone over the chasm and jumps himself.
:::

Every roll will either be a flat Stat roll, in which case you just roll the Stat (e.g. “roll **Logic, DC8**”) or an Ability, which includes both the Ability rank and the associated Stat (e.g. “roll **Divination, DC14**”). Your character sheet will tell you which Stat is associated with the Ability in question.

Some effects and abilities will change your degrees of success directly. For example, you may be under the effect of a spell that gives you 1 more degree of success on checks to Resist Wound (a **Body** roll), in which case, you would simply add 1 degree of success or subtract 1 degree of failure.

The effect will specify whether it is able to convert a failure into a success or vice-versa—if so, adding a degree of success to 1 degree of failure will result in 1 degree of success or vice-versa, and if not, adding a degree of success to 1 degree of failure will have no effect.

Some circumstances will make it possible to have degrees of success and failure at the same time. In this case, the result ends up as “success at a cost” or “failure with a silver lining,” where the result is a success but something bad happens at the same time, or the result is a failure but has some good to it anyway.

:::caption Example DC (Difficulty Class) Table
| **Easy** | 5 | **Extremely Hard** | 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Standard** | 10 | **Legendary** | 35 |
| **Challenging** | 15 | **Godlike** | 40 |
| **Difficult** | 20 | **Your GM is trying to tell you to do something else** | 50 |
| **Very Difficult** | 25 |   |   |

In summary, you say what you want to do, and the GM will either let you do it or make you roll to see how you do. If you roll, your success or failure is counted in blocks of five.

### Critical Successes and Failures

When you roll, if either die shows a 10, this is a **Critical Success.** Similarly, if either die shows a 1, this is a **Critical Failure.**

By themselves, critical successes and failures don’t necessarily have any properties. However, many effects rely on them—for example, the Heir’s **Unstable Power** ability increases the degrees of success on a check for each critical success, and increases the degrees of failure for each critical failure.

However, there are a few important properties:

1. **Resist:** If you roll to **Resist,** a critical success makes the roll a success no matter what the roll total is. Similarly, a critical failure makes the roll a failure no matter what the roll total is.
2. **Spell Backfire:** When rolling spells of Standard rank or higher, a critical failure triggers a backfire, which forces you to **Resist** a [condition](#conditions-and-resolve). More on this in the [Using Magic](#using-magic) section.
3. **Artifact Backfire:** Similarly to spell backfires, when using an artifact, a critical failure triggers a backfire, which can damage or even break the artifact. More on this in the [Artificy](#artificy) section.
4. ****Improvement Rolls:** On rolls to improve your abilities, critical successes count as a success always and increase the Stat associated instead of the ability itself. More on this in the [Improving your Abilities](#improving-your-abilities) section.**

There are other moves that can trigger backfires on critical failures similar to spells. The move will say something like “This move can trigger a backfire like a Standard spell” if so.

One in every fifty rolls gets a special result: a 10 on one die and a 1 on the other. This is an **Inflection Point.** Regardless of whether the check is a success or a failure, separately from the check altogether, something dramatic will happen when an **Inflection Point** is rolled, something that changes the dynamics of the scene.

If you roll an **Inflection Point,** the GM may even ask you to describe the nature of the **Inflection.** This dramatic result represents unexpected swells of power from the ley lines and the strange confluences of fate, so nothing is off the table when this happens.

## Improving Your Abilities

Now that you’ve tasted power, you probably want more.

You can improve your Ability ranks by using them. Just like in real life, you improve your abilities by attempting things that are just on the limits of your ability: if you **tie the DC, you get a chance to improve.**

When this happens, roll the Stat associated against **DC10 + your ranks in the Ability.** On success, increase the Ability that triggered this roll by 1.

:::dialog
**Kayla (Maya):** I know the professor left his journal somewhere here. Can I scour the place for it?
**Helena (GM):** Sure, roll **Search, DC20.**
**Kayla (Maya):** Twenty!? I swear, you’re mad at me.(Kayla rolls 2d10, getting a 7 and an 8, and adds Maya’s Focus, 2, and her Search rank, 3, for a 20.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Oh! It’s a twenty!
**Helena (GM):** Great! You scan through the room, pulling out drawers and crawling under furniture, and it’s when you’re putting a drawer back that you notice it rolls weirdly… you feel around inside and uncover a hidden cabinet that unlatches and creaks open slowly, and your fingers close on the tough leather binding of the professor’s journal, a thin layer of dust under your fingertips.
**Kayla (Maya):** This guy’s so sneaky. Can I read it?
**Helena (GM):** Hold on. You just tied the DC, so you get a chance to improve your **Search**. You have 3 ranks in **Search,** so roll **Focus, DC13.**(Kayla rolls 2d10, getting a 5 and a 7, and adds Maya’s Focus, 2, for a 14.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Fourteen!
**Helena (GM):** Nice! You feel a rush of adrenaline when your fingers come down on the book, a sense of satisfaction. Bump up Maya’s **Search** rank by 1.
:::

This improvement roll **cannot** trigger another improvement roll, even if you tie the DC. Sorry—endless learning is a lifelong process.

If either die shows a 10 on an improvement roll, this is a **Critical Improvement,** which increases the Stat associated by 1 instead of increasing the Ability. It’s customary to celebrate this with high-fives.

## Leveling Up

Every time you increase a Stat or an Ability, you gain a Rank Point, which can be used to increase your Class Rank.

You can purchase a new Class Rank at any time by spending a number of Rank Points equal to the rank you wish to purchase.

For example, Kayla is playing Maya, a rank-4 Rascal, and increasing her Search rank gave her a Rank Point, bringing her up to 4 Rank Points. However, because she is trying to purchase the 5th rank, she needs 5 Rank Points, meaning she’s still 1 short.

However, Ahmed is playing Carlos, a rank-5 Alchemist, and after a chase scene got a bit tight, he improved his Art skill (it’s a long story), bringing him up to 6 Rank Points, allowing him to purchase Alchemist Rank 6 and choose between the two class ability options.

You can also choose to purchase a new class at any time for 5 Rank Points, starting at rank 1. For example, after some more playing, Kayla has gotten to 5 Rank Points on Maya, and decides instead of purchasing the next rank of Rascal, she’d like to multiclass, and she picks up the Socialite class, having lied and charmed her way into celebrity status. Maya is now a rank-5 Rascal and a rank-1 Socialite, and picks a class ability option for Socialite rank 1, and now Kayla can choose whether she wants to save Rank Points to purchase Socialite rank 2, Rascal rank 6, or save all the way back up to 5 and add a third class.

Each class can only go up to rank 10. If you have two classes at rank 10, the GM will probably decide to cut you down to size, so beware.

## Contests

Not all rolls are made against a flat DC. Sometimes a roll will be made in direct competition with another person or creature, in which case both roll and compare their roll totals.

An example might be one character rolling **Deception** to lie against another person rolling **Read Person** to tell if it’s a lie, and if the **Deception** roll is higher, the lie is convincing. If the **Read Person** roll is higher, they see through the lie.

Mechanically speaking, this is almost identical to the DC being equal to the opposition’s roll, except for the case of a tie. In a flat DC check, if you roll an 18 on a DC18 check, it counts as one degree of success, but in a contest, it counts as a tie.

What a tie looks like is up to GM discretion, but usually looks like one of three options:

1. Nothing happens: the result is a stalemate somehow (e.g. an **Athletics** contest where two characters are trying to shove each other off a ledge, they tie and both stay standing on the ledge)
2. Mixed success for both: both sides are resolved as if having one degree of success and one degree of failure at the same time (e.g. a **Search—Stealth** contest where the character rolling **Search** discovers traces of the character rolling **Stealth,** but misconstrues the trail and follows it the wrong way)
3. Defender wins: the tie is resolved with a default success for whoever is trying to maintain things the way they are (e.g. an **Athletics** contest with one character trying to force open a door and the other trying to hold it shut, the door remains shut)

A tie in this way results in improvement rolls at the GM’s discretion, usually for one or both if both characters involved are PCs.

A contest can be asynchronous as well, where one roll occurs to set the DC and another roll is made against it later. For example, a character rolls **Hide Object** to place an amulet discreetly into a character’s dorm room, and then when the other character is looking for anything out of place in their room, they will roll **Search** against a DC equal to the **Hide Object** check.

In this type of contest, ties do not exist—because the first roll is completed beforehand, it sets a DC, and then the second roll is made against that DC. In the **Search—Hide Object** example, if the character rolling **Search** ties the **Hide Object** roll, they succeed and trigger an improvement roll for **Search.**

:::dialog
**Kayla (Maya):** I don’t like the look this guy is giving me. I’m going to cast Barrier and close him out.
**Helena (GM):** This guy is built like a tank, but I guess it’s worth a try. Roll your Barrier spell.(Kayla rolls a 3 and a 5 on the dice, and adds her character Maya’s Logic rank, 4, and her Warding rank, 7, for a 19.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Nineteen.
**Helena (GM):** Okay, that beats the Barrier DC of 16, so you create a barrier, but it’s a pretty flimsy one. You thrust your wand out with an incantation, and the translucent crystal lattice springs up covering the doorway. You see the guy’s expression darkening, studying the barrier, before he raises a fist and brings it down with a magic flare—
**Kayla (Maya):** I probably just antagonized him.
**Helena (GM):** He’s going to roll **Athletics** against your Barrier check.(Helena rolls for the muscleman, getting a 6 and a 10 on the dice, adding his Body rank, 5, and his Athletics rank, 5, for a 26.)
**Helena (GM):** That’s a 26 for him, which gives him two degrees of success. He smashes through the barrier, staggering forwards with the momentum, and he lurches forward and grabs Maya by the collar—
**Kayla (Maya):** I’m not letting this creep grab me! I push him away.
**Helena (GM):** You can roll **Athletics** against this guy and try to keep him away.(Helena rolls a 4 and a 2 for the strongman, adding 5 Body and 5 Athletics for a 16, while Kayla rolls an 8 and a 6 for Maya, adding 2 Body and 0 Athletics for a 16 as well.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Sixteen!
**Helena (GM):** He gets a sixteen too! That puts you at a tie, so… I’m going to say he doesn’t successfully grab you. He goes in for your neck, but you drop and squirm away, kicking and shoving to get him off of you, scrabbling away towards the back of the house. You’re fighting for your life right now, so give me an improvement roll for that.(Kayla rolls a 4 and a 5 for the improvement roll, adding 2 Body for an 11.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Yes! Eleven!
**Helena (GM):** Bump your **Athletics** rank up by one, then. You might need it.
:::

A contest may also be **forfeited** by one party, in case the target can’t or chooses not to resist, in which case the **forfeiting** party takes a 5 on their check.

For example, if Maya’s Telekinetic party member Finn rolls a 24 to cast **Levitate** on Maya to lift her to a location she’s trying to reach, instead of rolling to resist the effect, she can choose to **forfeit,** which means she takes a 5 on her check and fails with 4 degrees of failure, giving Finn 4 degrees of success’ worth of spell effect.

The GM may decide a contest is automatically forfeited, as well. For example, if Finn tries to cast **Elery’s Cannon** on a sleeping character, they won’t have a chance to dodge, and the GM decides the contest is thus **forfeited,** giving them a 5 without rolling for it.

This is not carte blanche to cast offensive spells on sleeping people, though. It’s still frowned upon.

## Action

Instead of combat, Starfall Academy has an alternate system called **Action,** which represents a broader spectrum of action scenes, since many Starfall campaigns have less focus on straight-up combat.

**Action** scenes can be many and varied, for example:

- An old structure starts to collapse as the players are inside
- The masked figure the players are trying to uncover is running away
- A bloody thrasher jumps the players
- A precious magic artifact is starting to break and needs to be saved
- That weird NPC you adopted 12 sessions ago just got stabbed and is bleeding out

In essence, any scene where every second counts can, at the GM’s discretion, be an **Action** scene. When **Action** starts, the GM will announce it, typically with fiendish glee, by telling the players to **roll for Action.**

An **Action roll** is a **DC10 Insight roll**. For each degree of success, take 1 Action Point (AP), up to a maximum of 3, and on failure, take 0. Action Points represent your opportunity to act, and you can never have fewer than 0 or more than 6.

:::dialog
**Kayla (Maya):** Okay, I’m going to just open the door.
**Ava (Finn):** We don’t want to check what the scratching inside is first?
**Kayla (Maya):** I am checking. By opening it. What’s inside?
**Helena (GM):** (trying not to smile) Maya walks confidently forward and pulls open the door, and immediately the scream of a dozen little ravenous creatures comes tearing out—
**Ava (Finn):** Not this *again,* Maya—
**Helena (GM):** Roll for **Action.**
**Kayla (Maya):** Ugh… just one time can it be something nice scratching?
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I rolled a 12.
**Ava (Finn):** No! I rolled a 4…
**Kayla (Maya):** 16!
**Helena (GM):** That’s 1 AP for Carlos, 2 for Maya, and zero for Finn. Maya is ready, already bracing herself when the door opens, while Finn is caught off-guard and screams, covering their face as the demonic creatures come pouring out from the scratched and torn-up doorway.
:::

**Action** is fast-moving and, instead of happening in turns, happens continuously at the GM’s discretion. Instead of waiting for your turn, you can spend AP at any time to take action, with some potential moves taking more AP than others. For example, moving away from a falling blade would only be 1AP, while attempting to sketch a picture of what’s going on would probably take 6AP (and get the GM to ask you why you want to do that).

If two players are both vying to use their AP at the same time, whichever one is using a lower AP cost goes first. If they’re using the same AP cost, whoever is closest to their target goes first. If one player yields precedence to the other, the players’ decision takes priority over everything else.

:::dialog
**Helena (GM):** The creatures come out in a flood, a horde of gnashing creatures with three heads that look like cow skulls, each creature barely higher than your ankle, but they come out in a wave, stinking of rotten meat. The flood of them pours towards Maya—
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I run away!
**Kayla (Maya):** Carlos! I’m dying here!
**Helena (GM):** Sure, you can use 1AP for a basic move and get away. You can put some distance between you and the creatures—
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Can I get behind the other empty house?
**Helena (GM):** Yeah, easily. You stumble backwards and sprint away, diving behind the rotting wood facade of the burned-out old house, and you’re down 1AP, to…?
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Zero. That’s okay. I’m safe here.
**Kayla (Maya):** Thanks, buddy. Can I cast Barrier before they’re all over me?
**Helena (GM):** Oh, good call. That 2AP out of the gate was a good reaction, so you can Barrier them inside the house if you succeed. Go ahead and roll it.
:::

Whenever something happens that potentially threatens a player, that is considered a Threat Move. Most Threat Moves are obvious: a griffin snaps at the player, a piece of the ceiling falls and nearly pins a player down, the angry wizard casts **Shatter** on someone’s face.

But sometimes the definition can be more vague—an **Action** scene where a magic construction is crumbling, and none of the players are actually in physical danger, but they can’t afford to let it fall apart. In these cases, a Threat Move is defined generally as **an event or action that threatens to put the players’ goal further out of reach.** The magic cracking out and toppling a tree that doesn’t risk hurting the players is dramatic, but isn’t a Threat Move, while a piece of the magic device the players are trying to protect breaking off and threatening to smash on the ground is definitely a Threat Move.

Every time a Threat Move takes place, every player involved in the **Action** scene gains 1AP, while any players that were directly threatened gain 2.

:::dialog
**Helena (GM):** The creatures are trapped inside by Maya’s Barrier, but they crash and claw at the Barrier, their tiny claws and tiny jaws leaving long scratch marks along the white lattice pattern. They’re going to roll to get through, and with those gnashing jaws, they’ll roll Strong, which, as a horde, is plus ten.
**Kayla (Maya):** Plus ten? *Helena.* I told you I’m sorry—(Helena rolls for the creatures, getting a 14 on the dice plus the 10 for their Strong roll, giving them a 24 that beats Maya’s Barrier roll.)
**Helena (GM):** That’s a 24, which shatters Maya’s Barrier. The crashing sound of it echoes hauntingly through the valley, the horde of them pouring out across the ground. You each take 1AP for the Threat Move—
**Kayla (Maya):** Don’t I take 2? They targeted my Barrier.
**Helena (GM):** They targeted your Barrier, not you. You get 2 if you get directly targeted yourself.
**Kayla (Maya):** Ugh—
**Helena (GM):** Don’t worry, though. They’re directly targeting you now.
**Kayla (Maya):** That’s not what I wanted—
**Helena (GM):** Three of them come at you at once, two tackling your legs and chomping at you, one jumping off from where it climbed up the door. We’ll contest their Strong roll versus your **Agility** to keep away from them.(Helena rolls a 20 for the creatures, while Kayla rolls a lucky 22 with only a +3 to **Agility**.)
**Helena (GM):** That’s a 20—
**Kayla (Maya):** 22! Yes!
**Helena (GM):** Oh, nice! You’re not normally good at this, but right now you’re operating purely on adrenaline, fear pumping your blood in your veins, and you bat them away with a scream that tears itself up out of your throat, backing away from the flood of gnashing jaws. They don’t get you this time, and that’s 2AP for you, 1AP for the others.
**Ava (Finn):** I have 2AP, I’m going to cast **Elery’s Cannon**—
**Kayla (Maya):** No, I have 3AP, these are magically animated creatures, I’m going to cast **Nullity Field**!
**Helena (GM):** **Nullity Field** is an advanced spell, so Finn’s spell gets to go first—
**Ava (Finn):** No, Maya can go first. This is her problem anyway.
**Helena (GM):** Sounds like you have some issues to work out. Kayla, go ahead and roll for Maya’s **Nullity Field**.
:::

**Action** ends when the scene comes to a close—the hunter demon is banished, the players escape from the fire trap, the weird NPC is saved, or maybe the villain escapes with the stolen potion or the players are all Downed. All AP are cleared at the end of **Action**—they can’t be carried over to the next **Action** scene.

At the GM’s discretion, they may ask anyone to make an improvement roll at the end of **Action** for any relevant Ability.

:::dialog
**Kayla (Maya):** Yes! 24 for **Nullity Field**!
**Helena (GM):** You slash your wand down with a scream that rips from your lungs, wrenching up all the magic you have in you—it explodes outwards, down your arm and out your wand, spilling out in a silvery ring around you, and it lights up in a brilliant silver glow across the ground. The creatures are demonic entities, and with a chorus of tiny screams, all the ones inside the **Nullity Field** are banished, dissolving instantly into a black smoke, while the rest of them tumble inside, charging forward like lemmings to be banished by the field’s antimagic, until the last one trips and falls across the **Nullity Field** barrier and is dispelled in a puff of smoke like the others, leaving you all in a heavy, breathless pause, waiting to see if anything else happens, if anything else comes out of the rotten stench in the house.
**Ava (Finn):** Let me guess, something does.
**Helena (GM):** Actually, no, this time. The valley is quiet. **Action** is over.
**Kayla (Maya):** Oh, thank God—
**Helena (GM):** That was a really clever **Nullity Field**. Give me an improvement roll for **Warding**.(Kayla rolls a 1 and a 3, adding Maya’s Logic rank of 4 for an 8.)
**Kayla (Maya):** Eight. Dammit. I’m not getting any wins today.
:::

Common moves have standardized AP costs. For example, the AP costs of spells depend on their levels (1AP for Basic up to 4AP for Legendary spells), while others depend more on GM discretion. Keep in mind that these can be combined—you are free to use them at any time as long as you have the AP, so you could, say, use simple movement for 1AP and cast a standard spell for 2AP at the same time for 3AP total.

Any action can be performed stealthily, adding 1 to the AP cost. When performed stealthily, any other characters who don’t have a reason to be looking for you won’t notice you. If another character has some reason to suspect you’re there and wants to look for you, roll **Stealth** against their **Perception.**

:::caption Common AP Costs
| **0AP (Free)** | Talking, drawing a wand, looking around that doesn’t involve **Search** or **Perception** |
| --- | --- |
| **1AP** | Basic spells, simple movement (running a short distance, climbing an easy slope), easy skill checks |
| **2AP** | Standard spells, standard movement (running a long distance, jumping, climbing, swimming), most skill checks, taking a potion |
| **3AP** | Advanced spells, complicated movement (climbing or swimming at high speed, carrying something heavy through an athletic task, difficult climbs), challenging skill checks, giving somebody else a potion |
| **4AP** | Legendary spells |
| **+1AP** | Acting stealthily |

## Conditions and Resolve

Instead of HP, characters in Starfall have a score called Resolve, which represents the character’s ability—physical and mental—to keep going.

Every player character has a maximum of 5 Resolve, and when it hits 0, the character is Downed, which represents the character being defeated, in a variety of ways (more on that later). A character loses Resolve by taking Conditions, meaning a healthy character has 0 Conditions and 5 Resolve, all the way down to 5 Conditions and 0 Resolve.

There are 5 different types of Condition, and a player can have up to 3 stacks of each one. The Conditions are detailed in this table.

:::caption Conditions Reference Table
| **Condition** | **Description** | **Resisted With** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fear** | Abject terror. Characters Downed with this Condition are likely to run away. | **Logic** |
| **Despair** | Hopelessness, a sense that the battle cannot be won. Characters Downed with this Condition may hide, turn away from the fight, give up. | **Insight** |
| **Wound** | Physical injuries. Characters Downed with this Condition may be knocked unconscious or, if the player consents, even killed. | **Body** |
| **Loss** | Sadness, personal loss, grief, self-doubt and a sense of loneliness. Characters Downed with this Condition likely lose their will to fight. | **Creativity** |
| **Doubt** | Doubt in the cause, a sense that maybe the character is on the wrong side. Characters Downed with this Condition may give up or even side with the enemies. | **Focus** |

Because it takes 5 Conditions to Down a character and each character can only have up to 3 stacks of a given Condition, one Condition is not enough to Down them.

For example, Fear alone will not deter a character, but if a character at 3 Fear traversing the depths of the wild fey woods in the black of night sees someone they care about snatched away and dragged into a planar void, the sense of Loss incurred might push them over the edge to give up.

Whenever a character is at risk of suffering a Condition, they have the chance to **Resist** it. This is done with a flat Stat roll for the Stat associated with that Condition—for example, a character with high Creativity and Insight but low Logic would be resistant to Loss and Despair, but highly susceptible to Fear.

If the character succeeds on the **Resist** roll, they weather the blow and do not suffer any Conditions, but if they fail, then they are afflicted with one stack of the Condition in question, which can be a challenge to remove.

:::dialog
**Ava (Finn):** I scan my light spell over the clearing, see if I can track the shadow.
**Helena (GM):** You sweep your light spell across the clearing, looking out over the inky blackness, but your spell flickers out.
**Ava (Finn):** It does what?
**Helena (GM):** Instead, in the black gloom, the shadow appears to pull the light straight off the tip of the wand and consumes it…
**Kayla (Maya):** I think maybe we should have turned right at the fork.
**Helena (GM):** But in the last flicker before your light goes out, you see a hazy outline of something that’s almost human, there at the edge of the clearing, but standing too tall, too… lean. With pale, empty eyes that focus on Finn. Something about it—that one split-second glimpse of its hollow gaze—strikes cold, dead fear into your heart like an icy knife. **Resist Fear, DC12.**
**Ava (Finn):** I *said* we shouldn’t come out here—(Ava rolls a 3 and a 1 and adds Finn’s Logic rank, 2, for a total of 6.)
**Ava (Finn):** Ugh. Six. Two degrees of failure. Don’t tell me I take two stacks.
**Helena (GM):** Not this time. You take a stack of Fear, the cold tendrils of that stare clutching like claws around your heart, and for your second degree of failure, I’ll say… the fear roots you to the spot, freezing you in place, your heart pounding in your ears suddenly the only thing that exists.
**Ava (Finn):** Guys—a little help?
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I’m going to pick them up, carry them to safety.
**Helena (GM):** Carlos is… bleeding out. Do you want to do that anyway?
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Eh, yeah. What’s the worst that could happen?
**Helena (GM):** Gutsy. Roll **Athletics, DC19.**
**Ahmed (Carlos):** (rolls) Uh—fourteen.
**Helena (GM):** That’s two degrees of failure, so you blow it. You strain, rushing forwards, and try to scoop up Finn, but the weight puts a sudden strain on the cut in your midsection, pain flaring up and lacing through your body. **Resist Wound, DC14.**(Ahmed rolls a 7 and a 5 and adds Carlos’s Body rank, 4, for a total of 16.)
**Ahmed (Carlos):** Sixteen! I’m good!
**Helena (GM):** You wince, bearing the pain as best you can, and it’s not slowing you down—
**Ahmed (Carlos):** I’m going to try again to carry Finn.
**Kayla (Maya):** For God’s sake, man. I’m going to use magic. Stop bleeding on them.
:::

**Resist** rolls do not trigger improvement rolls.

### Downing

If you hit 0 Resolve, your character is Downed. This represents, in an abstract sense, your character being defeated.

If this happens, work with the GM to decide what this looks like for your character, taking into account what type of Conditions they’re experiencing. A character Downed by Doubt and Loss is likely to look different than one Downed by Fear and Wound.

Downing is never fatal unless agreed between the GM and the player otherwise—if you get Downed in dramatic fashion and you think your character arc is served by your character dying there, communicate that to the GM and work out what your Downing might look like.

Sometimes your character might walk off a Downed effect—someone Downed by Wound gets a chance to heal it off and gets back in the fight, for example. But Downing is dramatic and potent, and can sometimes represent a drastic change for your character.

For example, Ava’s character Finn is at 3 Fear after an encounter gone horribly wrong deep in the Buried Forest, and when a fight goes badly and inflicts 2 Wound, they are Downed. Their friends get them out safely and restore them to health, but Ava decides that Finn is shaped by the experience, carrying a nasty scar from the incident and a deep-seated fear of the Buried Forest, and they refuse to enter it again. Ava may decide for Finn to overcome their fear over the course of the story.

Sometimes the effect might be as dramatic as that you decide your character dies, or even betrays their friends, going to the other side, which might look like giving up control of your character to the GM and making a new character. Everything should be decided between you and your GM, and it should serve your character’s storyline.

### Restoring Resolve

Taking Conditions is no small event, which means that restoring them is no walk in the park, either. A stack of Fear isn’t just seeing a gross bug (although that’s pretty scary), but is a deep, existential fear that makes you question if you can even continue. A stack of Wound isn’t just a bump or a bruise, but a significant injury that might keep you from carrying on.

In other words, if you’re down at 1 Resolve, it’s going to be a battle getting back up to 5.

The simplest way to clear Conditions is rest. A good night’s sleep, while being properly fed and safe from whatever is causing the Conditions, will restore 1 Condition stack of your choice. Starfall Academy, like every other magic academy, has an infirmary wing that will, excepting extraordinary circumstances like maybe that you’re in a deathmatch with the head nurse, always provide the ideal circumstances to clear 1 Condition stack per night of rest.

If you use [Downtime](#downtime) to rest, this will also clear a Condition stack.

Restoration, Herbalism, and Alchemy can all provide bonuses to help clear Conditions more effectively, but they each have their limits too.

If your character is Downed, they won’t be able to do anything (except maybe crawling back to the infirmary) until they’re given the chance to restore at least 1 Resolve.

Ultimately, the best way to avoid getting Downed is to not suffer Conditions in the first place, and to stay somewhere safe and not get into trouble. But I don’t think you’re going to listen to that advice.

## Using Magic

Magic is the backbone of the Starfall system. Your wand is your lifeline, and the spells you know define how you experience the world. And of course, they give you great power, and of course, they have a cost.

Because where’s the fun without a little cost?

Spells come in 5 different levels:

1. Basic
2. Standard
3. Advanced
4. Legendary
5. Hex

Most of the time, hopefully, you won’t be casting spells in category 5.

Regardless of the spell level, each spell is associated with a given Subject, and almost always involves rolling against a DC. When you want to use a spell, you roll for the associated Subject and compare to the DC. For example, a character casting the Chronomancy spell **Echoes of the Past** would need to roll **Chronomancy, DC13,** which will be 2d10+Insight rank+Chronomancy rank.

### Basic Spells

Basic spells (often called “flickers” by most students of magic) are spells that are simple to cast and difficult to get wrong. This category of spell can be cast an unlimited number of times without cost.

Basic spells typically have some kind of limiter built into the spell itself—for example, the Divination spell **Visions** gives information about an object, but contains the caveat, “You can only use this spell once on any given object.”

These spells are ones you can cast without any worry or thought. Sometimes, at the GM’s discretion, you may not even need to roll for them.

### Standard Spells

The majority of spells you will probably be casting are in this category. These are your bread-and-butter spells to get things done, often encapsulating the most important aspects of a given field of magic.

There is no cost to casting a Standard spell, but there is an important caveat: when you roll a Standard spell, **if either of the dice shows a 1 (a critical failure), you must Resist a Condition.** This is called a **backfire,** and can happen even if the spell check is a success, as it represents the magic drawing your essential life force out to power it.

The GM will decide, based on the circumstances, which Condition is most appropriate to **Resist**. The DC of the **Resist** check is always at the GM’s discretion, but is usually set by **the DC of the spell itself - 4,** as long as there is such a spell DC to go by.

:::dialog
**Ava (Finn):** Okay, I’ve had it. I cast **Elery’s Cannon.**
**Helena (GM):** On the squirrel?
**Ava (Finn):** On the squirrel.
**Helena (GM):** Right then. That’s a **DC17 Telekinesis** roll.(Ava rolls a 1 and a 9 and adds Finn’s Focus, 5, and their Telekinesis rank, 7, for a 22. Because of their **Wandjock** rank ability **Signature Trick,** they add their **Wandjock** rank of 4, for a total of 26.)
**Ava (Finn):** 26. With a backfire.
**Helena (GM):** Let’s go ahead and resolve your backfire first. **Elery’s Cannon** is an explosively powerful spell, so I’m thinking Wound, but since you’re just casting it on a… squirrel… we’ll keep the stakes lower and make it DC5. **Resist Wound, DC5.**(Ava rolls a 1 and a 7, adding Finn’s Body rank of 2 for a total of 10, but because they rolled a 1, the roll is a **critical failure**, meaning it’s at least 1 degree of failure.)
**Ava (Finn):** Ugh… critical failure. 1 degree of failure.
**Helena (GM):** Ouch. Okay, so a 26 definitely casts the spell successfully, even if the blowback from the spell knocks you clear over backwards and sends a stabbing pain up your arm. Mark one stack of Wound. The squirrel is going to roll Strong for Agility, but its Strong roll is, like, a two, so…(Helena rolls for the poor squirrel.)
**Helena (GM):** Okay, the squirrel gets a 7, which is 4 degrees of failure. I’ll give it a minus three to Resist for each degree of failure, and it’s probably rolling Weak on **Resist Wound, DC22**, so, um…(Helena rolls for the poor squirrel.)
**Helena (GM):** All right, that’s a negative six. That’s six degrees of failure. You launch a piece of scrap metal at the squirrel, and it nearly breaks your arm, but it launches the squirrel into low-earth orbit, where it will never bother you again. I guess you win?
:::

Some spells will force a character to **Resist** a given Condition, whether the caster, the target, or someone else. If the spell does not specify the **Resist** DC, it is established like a backfire, subtracting 4 from the number (in this case, the spell check of 26). In this example, the standard backfire DC for **Elery’s Cannon** would be 13, but Helena decided, based on the context, that it should be lower for Finn since it’s on a small target, but had the squirrel face the standard target DC of 22.

Unfortunately for the squirrel.

### Advanced Spells

Advanced spells are more powerful spells out of the reach of ordinary spellcasters, and not entirely suited for day-to-day interactions. These spells represent the higher realizations of the various fields of magic and their extended forms, and will have dramatic impacts when cast.

In addition to the risk of backfire like in a Standard spell, an Advanced spell requires **materials**, which are covered more in [Crafting and Materials](#crafting-and-materials), but essentially represent your resources for magical activities. Every Advanced spell carries the same material cost: 500. The GM may refund some of the cost for higher degrees of success, but the cost will not go higher. If the spell fails, half the material cost is refunded, giving you back 250.

If you have fewer than 500 materials, you cannot cast any Advanced spells until you do, no matter how nicely you ask.

### Legendary Spells

If this section is even relevant to you, congratulations.

Legendary spells represent the apex of what is possible with a given field of magic. They are extremely difficult to even find the barest guidance on how to cast them, let alone to actually cast. These include spells to circumvent death, tap into millennia-old power, and rewrite the very fabric of reality, so it’s not exactly something you cast for a little fun on a Sunday morning.

Legendary spells carry the risk of backfire, cost 2000 materials, and each one also requires a specific material that isn’t easy to get your hands on and is used up in the spell’s casting, even if the spell fails. If the spell fails, half the materials are refunded, giving you back 1000.

### Hexes

Please don’t.

Okay, so you might anyway. Hexes are forbidden magic—distinct from curses, which is just using regular magic to hurt people, hexes are a completely separate type of magic whose sole purpose is to cause strife and harm. You are not supposed to cast these. You will get in a LOT of trouble. You’re not supposed to even know how to cast them, let alone actually cast them.

Hexes have varying AP costs, specified in the spell description. Every hex costs 500 materials per AP cost, and because of the twisted magics that go into casting them, **every hex roll is treated as a backfire.** Materials are **not** refunded on failure.

Also, if authorities find out that you’re casting a hex, you’re going to get into a lot of trouble. I mean a lot of trouble. I really mean it, you shouldn’t do it.

Please don’t do it.

You’re not going to do it, right?

### Rituals

Some spells have the Ritual tag, such as the Enchantment spell **Repair Enchantment**. These spells can be cast two ways: regularly, or as a ritual.

If cast regularly, it behaves the same as a spell without the Ritual tag—in the case of **Repair Enchantment,** as a Standard spell.

If cast as a ritual, the spell takes one hour to cast, but then behaves as one level lower—in the case of **Repair Enchantment,** as a Basic spell, which removes the chance of backfiring that exists with a Standard spell.

Advanced spells can also have the Ritual tag, which allows them to be cast, taking an extra hour, as a Standard spell, removing the need for material costs.

### Volatile Spells

Some spells are marked as Volatile, such as the Demonology spell **Demonic Communion** and most Crystallomancy spells. These spells always trigger a backfire no matter their spell level.

If you cast a Volatile spell as a ritual, it is no longer treated as volatile as well as decreasing the spell level as usual.

## Wands

Every student has a wand, or they’re in trouble.

A wand is the conduit you cast magic through. They come in many different forms with many different bonuses, but they all let you do the same thing: cast magic.

When you cast spells, including to place enchantments, if you do not have a wand in your hand, you must take a -8 penalty to the roll.

That said, barehanded casting has the benefit of being subtle—waving a wand around makes it clear what you’re doing, and spellcasters who are very familiar with the spell you’re using might be able to recognize what spell you’re about to cast from the precise wand gestures used, so even if you have a wand on hand, you might want to opt for a barehanded spell. It’s up to you.

You can only have 1 wand equipped at a time, and you can only change your active wand once every 8 hours. You can, however, carry as many unequipped wands with you as you like. (The GM might have questions if you’re carrying hundreds of them around, though.)

Certain circumstances might damage your wand—a success and a failure on a spell, a dramatic Inflection Point, a particularly bad failure on a spell, a crystal palace stepping on it, and any other number of factors. A wand’s condition is represented in materials: a wand might cost 800 materials at maximum and be damaged down to a current status of 400 materials.

A damaged wand can still be used, but if it hits 0 materials, it becomes unusable until repaired by a wandcrafter. More on that in the [Wandcrafting](#wandcrafting) section.

## Crafting and Materials

So, you’ve got a knack for creation. Better making things than breaking things, is what Starfall faculty always says. Especially cleaning staff.

You have **Materials** as a resource representing your general wealth of magic materials available for casting spells, brewing potions, placing enchantments, and making and maintaining artifacts. They can also function like a currency among students—a cunning alchemist might take to brewing a recipe only they know that takes 700 materials to make and selling it off in exchange for 1000 materials, keeping their material reserves high, or a student who has a powerful old tome they don’t need might be persuaded to trade it in if offered enough materials.

Materials are used in one of six ways: spellcasting, enchantment, alchemy, runology, wandcrafting, and artificy. They’re detailed in the sections below, along with the last of the creation magics: herbalism, which often helps you get materials instead.

### Spellcasting Materials

Casting Advanced or higher-level spells requires the use of materials, as detailed in this table:

| **Spell Level** | **Materials Cost** | **Returned on Failure** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Advanced** | **500** | **Half** |
| **Legendary** | **2000** | **Half** |
| **Hex** | **500 * AP Cost** | **None** |

### Enchantment

Placing an enchantment on an object allows you to transfer some properties of the spell into the object. Some are obvious—an invisibility spell placed on a sword makes an invisible sword, and a flight spell on a Funko Pop makes a flying Funko Pop, if you want one, for some reason. But some aren’t as obvious—the Necromancy spell **Amalgamate** to combine body parts together into one animated hybrid monster, enchanted onto your coffee machine, it’s hard to say what that would do.

When you enchant a spell onto an object, work with your GM to determine what that looks like. The GM can rule that a certain spell simply cannot be enchanted onto a certain object, or possibly even that it cannot be enchanted onto anything. However, oftentimes, the result will just be odd and unpredictable—maybe your GM rules that your **Amalgamate** coffee machine fuses itself with your other kitchen appliances and forms a shambling mound of kitchenware that follows you around. Enchanters have done weirder.

In order to enchant a spell onto an object, you must have cast the spell at least once before. If it has been a very long time since you have, the GM may rule that you have to cast it again. If you have cast the spell before, then you may **Enchant** it onto a nonliving object. Roll **Enchantment** against the DC of the spell you wish to enchant onto an object, and on success, the object will take on properties aligning with the spell for an amount of time depending on your degrees of success:

| **Degrees of Success** | **Time Enchanted** |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **1 hour** |
| 2 | **1 day** |
| 3 | **1 week** |
| 4+ | **Until dispelled** |

Any time you choose to enchant a spell onto an object, you must spend **100 materials**, and also any requisites you would need for the spell itself:

- For a Standard spell, your roll to **Enchant** will backfire on a 1
- For an Advanced spell, you may backfire and you must spend 500 further materials
- For a Legendary spell, you may backfire and you must spend 2000 further materials, and the special materials
- For a Hex, you backfire and you must spend 500 further materials per AP cost
- If there are any specific requisites listed in the spell description, they may apply as well at GM discretion

Objects you have enchanted will generally follow your directions. Generally.

### Alchemy

Potions are great, because it’s like using magic and cracking open a nice cold soda at the same time. They usually taste pretty rancid, but, you know, you can’t win them all.

Every potion has its own materials cost and an **Intensity Rank** describing how difficult it is for the two important activities with potions: brewing them and using them. Let’s start with the simpler side, using them.

#### Taking Potions

Potions aren’t just pretty things in pretty bottles. They’re most helpful when you drink them.

Once you take a potion, you get all of its effects immediately, with no drawbacks. However, you cannot take another potion until you have gotten the first one out of your system—until you have **Metabolized** it.

When you drink a potion, perform a **Metabolize** roll, which is an **Alchemy** roll against the potion’s **Intensity Rank.** The result will determine how long you have to wait before you can take another potion.

| **DEGREES OF SUCCESS** | **METABOLIZE TIME** |
| --- | --- |
| **-3 OR LESS** | **1 WEEK** |
| -2 | **3 DAYS** |
| -1 | **24 HOURS** |
| 1 | **12 HOURS** |
| 2 | **3 HOURS** |
| **3 OR MORE** | **1 HOUR** |

If you try to drink another potion before the metabolize time is up, the GM will warn you it won’t work, and if for some reason you decide to do it anyway, you’ll just throw up and not gain any benefits from the new potion. Plus, potion vomit is just really hard to get out of your clothes.

#### Brewing Potions

When you set out to brew a potion, you need an **Alchemy Lab.** You can always access free-use alchemy labs on campus, but anything you brew at a public alchemy lab is public record, so don’t make anything there you don’t want people to know about.

If you don’t have access to an alchemy lab, you can jury-rig one by rolling **Alchemy** against a DC set by the GM depending on how much access you have to the parts you would need (anywhere from DC10 to DC25). On success, you spend 2 hours jury-rigging an alchemy lab that will work for 500 materials’ worth of brewing per degree of success.

Once you have access to an alchemy lab, you can brew potions. You can only brew potions you know the recipes for and have enough materials to afford the cost for. Once you do, roll **Alchemy** against the **Intensity Rank** of the potion you want to make, and on success, you can start making the potion.

Potions take time to brew, though, and take longer for more material costs. If you put in hands-on time, every 1 hour you spend completes 100 materials’ worth of brewing, and for hands-off time, every 1 day spent brewing completes 500 materials’ worth of brewing.

You can switch between the two for more prolonged brewing periods. As long as nobody disrupts your alchemy lab or tries to sabotage your potion, it can stay brewing indefinitely until finished.

You can obtain potion recipes through [Alchemy classes](#daily-life), [Downtime activities](#downtime), or regular gameplay. Once you’ve learned a recipe, you know it forever.

### Runology

Runes are often dismissed as being slow, clunky, and stuffy scholarship over practicality. However, slow, clunky, and stuffy scholarly types love them, so really, take your pick.

Runes are an ancient, fundamental language of magic itself that can manifest magic effects into reality. A rune is made up of [glyphs](/compendium?tab=Glyphs), runic symbols that carry a small piece of distinct magic energy, formed into a self-contained circle of magic that we know as the rune, and then these runes can be combined with one another for extended magic effects.

In essence, the glyphs are words, each rune a self-contained sentence, and those sentences can be strung together to tell a story.

But only if you’re a huge nerd.

A rune on any surface will activate its magic. Runologists often have piles and piles (and more piles) of runic paper, a cheap and plentiful material that can be used to draw a rune onto and activate its magic without placing the rune’s magic into a surface or an object, but runes can also be placed onto anything, which can affect the object they’re placed on.

Creating runes will allow you to inscribe and chart magic into the world around you, but keep in mind that creating magic with runes is slower than by any other method. It’s not exactly for tossing out magic in the heat of the moment.

When you set out to create a rune, choose what you want to place it on, whether that’s runic paper or another nonreactive surface, or if it’s on an object you want to be affected by the rune, and then choose the glyphs from ones you know that you want to use to create a rune.

When you do, work out with the GM what the effect looks like based on the glyphs you use. Keep in mind that the effect won’t always be exactly what you intend—the history of runology is one of runes with unintended effects. For example, you may intend for **Soothe-Fear-Fey** to guard against fear inflicted by fey, only to find it instead restores and heals terrifying fey creatures instead.

In general, the GM will likely make the end result closer to what you want the more degrees of success you have on your **Runology** check to create the rune, but you’ll typically be using multiple glyphs to try to narrow down the effect you want. For instance, trying to set up a rune to create a sonic burst of destruction, you may find **Shout-Weapon** ends up in the “leadership” interpretation of **Shout** and makes a rallying-banner effect instead, but using **Shout-Weapon-Wrath** produces the desired sonic-attack effect.

When choosing glyphs, you can use as many as you like, but you must use at least two. You can use the same glyph multiple times in the same rune, but you probably don’t want to inscribe a **Demon-Demon-Demon-Demon-Demon** rune on your bed.

Creating a rune costs materials and has an associated **Intensity Rank.** These are listed underneath the name of the glyphs: for example, a glyph might read 40 2 underneath it, which means it costs 40 materials and has an **Intensity Rank** of 2. To get the materials cost and **Intensity Rank** of a rune, add together the cost and rank of each glyph in the rune.

When you create a rune, spend the materials and roll **Runology** versus the rune’s **Intensity Rank.** If you succeed, the rune can be created in an amount of hours equal to the rune’s **Intensity Rank,** minus 4 for each degree of success on the check.

For example, if you roll 25 to create a rune with an **Intensity Rank** of 20, it takes 20 hours minus 8 for the two degrees of success on your check, meaning it will take 12 hours. This time doesn’t need to be consecutive, and you can spend a **Downtime** block to make 8 hours’ worth of progress. If a rune would take less than 1 hour, it instead takes 30 minutes.

If you fail the **Runology** check, your materials are not refunded, but you can spend an hour to repeat the **Runology** check on the same rune without spending the materials again, to attempt to pick up the pieces and give the rune another try.

The glyphs are listed in [the Glyphs section](/compendium?tab=Glyphs).

Once you create a rune, it will last until it is dispelled. You can also choose, when making the rune, to make the rune always active, or set up activation criteria, in which case the rune will do nothing until the criteria are met, at which point it will activate until the criteria are no longer met.

You can only use the glyphs you know. When you create a character, you will take 2 glyphs for each point you have in **Runology,** and the GM may award extra glyphs to you as you explore the world. You can also spend a **Downtime** block researching runes and roll **Runology, DC5**, learning 2 glyphs for each degree of success, one of your choice and one chosen at random by the GM.

Keep in mind that runes are so expansive that they can unexpectedly imbalance a game. If your GM needs to retcon what your rune does exactly, be cooperative and pretend they never said anything different. And keep in mind that you alone are responsible for whatever comes from that brilliant rune idea you have.

### Wandcrafting

If you want to get everyone to like you, Wandcrafting is a good way to get there.

Wandcrafters have a reputation for being the friendliest and most outgoing, which gives impostor syndrome to wandcrafters who aren’t that. Because wandcrafters often work in wandcrafting studios with a few other people they develop close, meaningful bonds with, and they tend to work very closely hands-on with a person to be able to craft a wand for them, they generally have a few friends everywhere.

If you want to craft a wand, you’ll typically need access to a studio. If you’re taking any wandcrafting classes, you’ll be assigned a studio with a few other wandcrafters, typically three to five others, and you can use it freely, although any wands you craft in this studio are on public record, so don’t make anything there that you don’t want people to know about.

You can also forgo a studio, but you’ll take a -8 penalty to any **Wandcrafting** rolls done without a studio that normally need one.

When you set out to build a wand, you need to cast it for the person who will be using it. Depending on how close you are with the person, you will take anywhere from a -6 penalty to a +6 bonus on **Wandcrafting** rolls to create the wand, at the GM’s discretion. You can make a wand for yourself, but you cannot get a bonus on the roll if you do.

Once you’ve selected the person you’re casting the wand for, pick the wand you want to create, and say how many hours you want to work for. Roll **Wandcrafting, DC5,** and spend 20 materials per degree of success, per hour, towards the wand’s material cost. You can repeat this as many times as you like, and once you’ve spent the entire material cost of the wand, the wand is finished, and it is bound to the person you created it for, preventing anybody else from using it.

They can choose to unbind the wand after having been bound to it for a week, but once unbound, a wand cannot be bound again. A wand can be unbound at any time without taking any time or effort, after which anybody can use it just by picking it up and activating it.

You can see the list of wands available in [the Wands section](#wands).

### Artificy

An artifact is a precious, powerful magic item that binds with a person’s soul in a process called **Attunement** so that only that person can use it. This connection is intense and personal—any artifact will have some presence of its own, almost like a living being, and because of the intensity of this bond, a person can only be **Attuned** to 3 artifacts at once.

This number can be changed: for every 5 ranks in Artificy, a character can attune to 1 more artifact, and some abilities give additional attunements.

You can find the base list of artifacts in [the Artifacts section](/compendium?tab=Artifacts).

#### Attuning

Attuning to an artifact is no small feat. Reaching out and connecting your consciousness with that of the magical bastion that is an artifact is always going to be a draining experience.

Every artifact has an **Intensity Rank** that describes how difficult it is to forge and attune to. Whenever you attempt to attune to an artifact, unless it is a simple artifact, **you will probably fail.** Plenty of artifacts have **Intensity Ranks** of 40 or higher and are an incredible feat even for an accomplished artificer to attune to on the first try.

Every time you fail an **Attune** roll, you must **Resist** a Condition chosen by the GM, with a DC equal to **5 * your Attune degrees of failure.**

However, repeated attempts can bring down the **Intensity Rank** as the artifact slowly begins to open up to you—or can also push it up—depending on how close you are to success.

Depending on your number of degrees of failure on the **Attune** roll, the **Intensity Rank** will change for you, potentially lowering it to the point where you can achieve it. However, higher numbers of degrees of failure will increase the **Intensity Rank** for you instead, meaning some are just out of your league until you improve at **Artificy.**

:::caption ATTUNING RESULTS
| **DEGREES OF FAILURE** | **INTENSITY RANK** | **DEGREES OF FAILURE** | **INTENSITY RANK** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **11 OR MORE** | +1 | 5 | -2 |
| 10 | 0 | 4 | -2 |
| 9 | -1 | 3 | -3 |
| 8 | -1 | 2 | -3 |
| 7 | -1 | 1 | -5 |
| 6 | -1 | **0 OR LESS** | **ATTUNED** |

You can keep track of the Intensity Rank of an unattuned artifact in your character sheet, under your inventory.

Changing the **Intensity Rank** of a given artifact only changes it for you specifically, as it represents the artifact’s growing bond with you. If anybody else attempts to attune to the artifact, they will have to start from its full **Intensity Rank.**

#### Damage and Repair

Artifacts are intensely magical, which can make them fragile. Whenever you roll to use an artifact and either die shows a 1, the artifact backfires, and you must succeed on a backfire check, a **DC16 Artificy** check, or have the artifact become **Damaged.**

While the artifact is **Damaged,** all checks made with the artifact are made at a -4 penalty, including backfire checks. If a **Damaged** artifact fails a backfire check, it becomes **Broken**, and it cannot be used until it is repaired.

A **Damaged** artifact can be fixed much more easily than a **Broken** one. Either way, the artificer can choose to attempt a fast, regular, or slow repair.

|   | **DAMAGED ARTIFACT REPAIR** | **BROKEN ARTIFACT REPAIR** |   |   |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ROLL TYPE** | **ARTIFICY DC** | **TIME TAKEN** | **ARTIFICY DC** | **TIME TAKEN** |
| **FAST** | 23 | **1 HOUR** | 28 | **12 HOURS** |
| **REGULAR** | 18 | **4 HOURS** | 23 | **24 HOURS** |
| **SLOW** | 12 | **8 HOURS** | 18 | **48 HOURS** |

These rolls can be spread over a long time period—for example, if the artificer wants to do a Slow repair for a Damaged artifact, they can do it in an 8-hour period or put in 2 hours a day over four days.

The artificer must decide the type of repair they want to undertake and then put in the total amount of time needed before they can roll the **Artificy** check against the **Repair** DC.

If they succeed on the **Repair** check, the artifact returns immediately to perfect condition. If they fail to repair a **Damaged** artifact, nothing happens, while if they fail to repair a **Broken** artifact, it still at least changes from **Broken** to **Damaged.**

If an artifact is left unused and unattended for a long period (at the GM’s discretion, but at least a month), it may become **Damaged,** but an artifact will never become **Broken** by disuse.

#### Creating an Artifact

Creating an artifact is a significant undertaking. Most of the time, you will obtain artifacts by finding them, but if you really want to impress your friends, you can make one instead.

Just keep in mind the process is so complicated that your GM might hate you.

To make a specific artifact, you will first need its **Schema.** Sometimes these are freely available—some Basic artifacts, you can find a **Schema** for it with some basic research, provided you have access to a library or other archive. For the majority of artifacts, however, getting your hands on a **Schema** will be a difficult undertaking.

Many **Schemas** are copyrighted by their creators and are protected fiercely as intellectual property. You may be able to purchase a limited copy to make an artifact for personal use, but artifact designers will likely upcharge you a fortune.

You could also steal one. Not that I would condone that! Remember, stealing is wrong.

You can choose to design a **Schema** of your own, but it isn’t easy. If you do decide to, spend 48 hours (nonconsecutively, unless you really want to do that to yourself) engaged in design work, and at the end, roll **Artificy, DC25.** If you succeed, describe roughly the type of artifact you want to create, and work with the GM to decide what artifact **Schema** comes out of it. It may be a **Schema** for an existing artifact, a modified version of an existing artifact, or a completely new artifact invented between the player and the GM, but you will have the **Schema** to create that artifact any time you like.

Once you have a **Schema**, you can take it to an **Artificy Workshop** to start making one. Starfall Academy and all other major magical institutions have public-access artificy workshops for their students and faculty, but anything you make there will be public record, linked back to you specifically, so probably don’t make anything illegal or with a stolen **Schema** at one.

If you don’t have access to a proper workshop, you can go without, so long as you have tools and materials, but the process will be significantly more difficult.

When you set out to create an artifact, you will need to provide the entire **Material Cost** upfront, and then work on it for a number of days equal to **its Intensity Rank / 5.** For example, an artifact with Intensity Rank 40 will need 8 days’ worth of work.

A day of work assumes 4 hours of uninterrupted work. If you cannot put in 4 hours of uninterrupted work, it doesn’t yield any progress (artifacts are fickle). You can put in multiple blocks of 4-hour work in a given day to finish an artifact faster, up to a maximum of 4, but each successive block in a given day is more difficult than the last.

When you wish to work on an artifact, roll **Artificy** against the appropriate DC from the following table.

### Herbalism

Herbalism is dedicated to letting you use all the weird stuff you find in the woods, because what could go wrong with chewing on weird roots and snorting powders?

The wilds are full of magic plants—and a lot of regular ones. When you explore wild areas, most of the plants you’ll be picking past are either ordinary or low-level enough magic that they don’t have any particular properties, but occasionally you’ll be able to come across a **magic plant**, which are detailed in [the Plants section](/compendium?tab=Plants). These are plants that have enough magic to have interesting properties—and even better, material value.

When you encounter a **magic plant,** you’ll be able to tell immediately that it is one, but you won’t necessarily know what kind it is. You can roll Herbalism versus its **Intensity Rank,** which is described in its Appendix entry, and on success, you’ll be able to tell all its properties, and you can choose what to do with it.

If you’ve identified a **magic plant,** you can choose to harvest it, which gives you materials equal to its **Material Value** and destroys the plant, or you can use its **Ability,** which represents what the magic of the plant can do for you. For example, the plant **Herrera’s Silk Snag** allows you to pick 250 materials from it once a day, and unless the **Ability** text says otherwise, using its **Ability** doesn’t consume the plant.

When you use a plant’s **Ability,** unless the **Ability** text says otherwise, you must roll versus its **Intensity Rank.** If an **Ability** only allows periodic use, such as once a day, then you must wait until the next day to try again, even if you fail, and you cannot harvest the plant until its **Ability** is ready for use again.

If you have ranks in **Herbalism,** you can also take **magic plants** away from where you found them and cultivate them. You can have any number of **magic plants** anywhere you like, but the combined **Material Value** of all the plants you are cultivating cannot exceed **50 times your Herbalism rank.** If you harvest, consume, sell, or otherwise lose possession of a plant in your cultivation, it will free up that part of your cultivation quotient.

Unless a plant’s **Ability** text specifies otherwise, you cannot bring a plant around with you—they need to be planted somewhere and not yanked around all the time. This is, like, gardening 101.

## Getting in Trouble

The most central thing in a student’s Starfall scholarship is magic. The second most central thing is getting in trouble.

As a Starfall student, it’s inevitable that you’ll get yourself into **Trouble** at one point or another. Because police aren’t equipped to handle magic, every academy has its own internal group to uphold magical law and order. They can be called different things in different academies, but at Starfall Academy and as a general term as well, they are referred to as the **Enforcers.**

Law enforcement turns a blind eye to what happens on academy campuses, trusting the academy’s Enforcer group to handle matters, which means that issues get resolved a little differently than if the police were handling them. Because any magic practitioner who falls out of the system invariably ends up causing more problems for everyone down the line, expulsion or imprisonment are almost unheard of as punishments issued by the Enforcers, preferring rehabilitative justice.

This isn’t because they’re good people, but because they’ve had too many experiences with what happens trying to lock up magic-users. It’s kind of just the practical route.

Because of all this, getting caught in the middle of a crime isn’t game over for your character, unless maybe you get found murdering people, in which case it’s probably just the GM telling you to please stop.

When you get found breaking the rules, you get in **Trouble.** The GM will decide, based on the magnitude of what you’re suspected of and on how likely you seem as a suspect, how much **Trouble** to award, generally 1 for a minor infraction up to 3 for a serious issue, but if you’ve done something truly heinous, you might get even more. Some people take it as a compliment.

You start out with your **Trouble** rank at 0, and it will max out at 10, but really, for your sake, let’s hope you don’t get there. As it increases, you will get more and more attention from the Enforcers trying to bring you to justice. You can decrease your **Trouble** rank in a variety of ways, such as providing evidence of your innocence, doing the Enforcers a favor, bribing someone in power, framing somebody else, or—the boringly practical option—facing the punishment for your crime.

Increasing your **Trouble** rank gets more and more difficult as you go higher, as your actions are held in the context of what you’ve already done. If you grab a rival’s wand and throw it into the river, you might go from 0 **Trouble** to 1 if the Enforcers find out you did it, but it’s unlikely to bump you up from 9 to 10 if you’ve already been burning down buildings, attacking senior officials, and organizing campus-wide rackets to steal millions of materials. They kind of don’t care about the guy’s wand at that point.

Does that mean you can get away with whatever you want as long as you’re already a criminal? I mean… I guess so. I shouldn’t be telling you that.

### Getting Caught

Having **Trouble** doesn’t mean immediately getting hauled in for justice. Lower ranks represent more that the **Enforcers** are annoyed with you than anything else. At higher ranks, they will make more active efforts to bring you to the **Enforcer’s Garrison,** the working base of the **Enforcers** and where they bring people for questioning, holding, and trials.

If you are brought into the **Garrison**, you can choose to face your punishment, in which case the **Enforcers** will deal a punishment appropriate to the amount of **Trouble** you have, and once it’s finished, your **Trouble** rank is reduced to 0.

That doesn’t mean they’ve forgotten about you, though. If you cleared a particularly high **Trouble** rank or are a repeat offender, they’ll likely be keeping a closer eye on you in the future, making it more likely that your acts will be caught and awarded **Trouble.**

The GM will choose an appropriate punishment if you get brought into the **Garrison**, using the table below as a suggestion—the punishment the GM chooses to issue will often be different than what it lists for your level. If your punishment is not imprisonment, you can choose to refuse your punishment, in which case you will be kept in holding in the **Garrison** until you either agree to face your punishment, take another method to reduce your **Trouble,** or you go with the old faithful route and break out of the **Garrison.**

Just keep in mind that’s not gonna lower your **Trouble** rank anytime soon.

### Escaping Trouble

The simplest way to reduce your **Trouble** rank is to get taken into the **Garrison** and face your punishment, which will reset you to 0.

But of course, you don’t want to do that, because you’re a little punk.

There are other routes you can take to reduce your **Trouble** rank, listed below. The GM may also lower or clear your **Trouble** rank altogether from gameplay, if appropriate—for example, if your **Trouble** rank was all wrapped up in a corrupt Enforcer Captain and you successfully bring the Captain’s corruption to light and get her removed, the GM may decide it’s appropriate to clear your **Trouble** rank as well.

1. ****Evidence:** You can present evidence to clear your name and prove you’re innocent—whether you actually are or not. The GM will decide whether it clears your name, reduces your **Trouble** rank, is overlooked, or, if they know you’re faking something, might even increase it. If you’re presenting false evidence, roll **Deception** against a DC set by the GM or against the **Read Person** or **Analyze** check of whoever you’re presenting to.**
2. ****Bribe:** An oldie but a goodie. If you have access to an Enforcer with authority over your case, you can attempt to bribe them. The GM will decide—possibly with a **Persuasion** roll versus their **Willpower** check—whether or not they’re receptive to the bribe, and if they are, how much of a bribe they would need to be offered. Bribe costs are typically in materials, but may be in other things, such as an artifact or a very rare and powerful potion. If they do not accept the bribe, this may instead increase your **Trouble** rank.**
3. ****Forge Evidence:** If you’re crafty, you can attempt to forge evidence “proving” your innocence, possibly by framing somebody else, creating an alibi, or just obfuscating the case. Describe what kind of evidence you would like to forge and how you would like to go about it, and roll **Improvise** against a DC set by the GM or against the **Investigate** check of whoever is going to discover your false evidence (whether that’s by you presenting it or by leaving it in a place where it will be found). If this fails, it may instead increase your **Trouble** rank. You can perform this with an amount of time the GM decides, typically an hour, or with a **Downtime** block, in which case you can take a +4 to your check.**
4. ****Doing Favors:** If you have access to an Enforcer with authority over your case, you can attempt to offer a little quid-pro-quo, doing a favor in exchange for dropping the case against you. This is unlikely to work at higher **Trouble** ranks, but if you have a low **Trouble** rank or if the Enforcer you’re offering to is in a tight enough space and desperate for help, the GM may give it to you or ask you to roll **Persuasion** versus the Enforcer’s **Willpower** to get them to cooperate. If they do, then they will not drop your **Trouble** rank right away, but will give you a task and a timeframe to accomplish it in, and until then, they’ll pretend not to see you. The task may be something to accomplish in gameplay or something you can complete in a **Downtime** block, at the GM’s discretion, but either way, if you complete it within the timeframe, your **Trouble** rank will decrease or clear altogether. If you don’t complete it within the timeframe or you do something that makes the situation worse, this may instead increase your **Trouble** rank.**

### Other Effects

Institutional reprimands don’t happen in a vacuum. When your **Trouble** rank gets higher, it will start to affect how people see you, for better or worse. If you have a very high **Trouble** rank, people may fear you outright, running away and calling the Enforcers at the sight of you, or they might panic and give you whatever you want.

Of course, it also depends on what exactly you’re infamous for. If you get into **Trouble** by gathering rebels and causing good trouble, people might fall in behind you, look up to you, and think more highly of you the more **Trouble** you manage to accumulate. If you get into **Trouble** by randomly beating up students you see, people will probably run away from you, because, you know, they think you might randomly beat them up.

These effects to your reputation tend to linger long after you’ve cleared your **Trouble.** Someone who’s gotten a reputation as a master thief and serves some time in the **Garrison**, coming out with a clear record, is still going to have people guarding their possession around them—and is still going to get requests for help when people want something stolen.

Your ability to engage with institutions will also change as you gain more **Trouble.** The infirmary will never refuse to treat a patient, not even the most hardened criminal, but if you have high **Trouble,** then the **Enforcers** will probably pay you a visit while you’re healing up.

Anything you do that goes on a public record, such as using a wandcrafting studio or a public alchemy lab, will also alert the **Enforcers** that you’re there if you have **Trouble.** Below 3 **Trouble,** they tend not to worry too much, but if you’re somehow rocking 10 **Trouble**, you shouldn’t use the public alchemy labs unless you want the entire force Shattering the door open behind you as soon as you touch the burner.

If you repeatedly get into **Trouble,** it will often be the same Enforcer or team of them assigned to your case, the **Enforcers** trusting that one as an expert in tracking down you specifically. Sometimes this leads into a homoerotic rivalry between Rascal and Enforcer. Tale as old as time, honestly.

## Duels

Aside from getting into trouble, one of the most popular activities for students is picking fights with one another.

**Duels** are a common sight around Starfall, whether as a way of resolving a dispute, friendly competition, training together, or just the closest you can legally get to breaking the neck of that annoying student who’s always starting things with you.

A **duel** is always between two people, and any intervention from other people invalidates the **duel**. To begin a **duel,** if both participants are willing, you can make a **wand oath,** crossing your wand with the opponent’s and, if applicable, making a promise on it—for example, promising that the loser will give the winner a certain number of materials, or that the loser will give up some information, or that the loser will perform some task. The promises can also be uneven, so that, say, one party can promise materials if they lose and the other will promise the answer to a certain question.

The **wand oath** binds your promise, and if you don’t hold to your end of the bargain after the **duel**, the magic will light up in a sigil on the back of your hand that anyone can recognize as a **duelbreaker’s mark,** signaling that you didn’t hold up to your end of a **duel** bargain. These **duelbreaker’s marks** go away after a few weeks, but the damage to your reputation will likely last—nobody wants to **duel** a **duelbreaker,** and it’s a symbol of dishonesty in general, which means people are less likely to trust you with anything.

When you start a **duel,** the rules are simple: force your opponent to touch the ground with anything other than the soles of their feet, including their wand. The GM may rule that this occurs based on what happens contextually in the setting—for example, maybe you shove them with a telekinetic spell that they fail against badly and it knocks them down instantly—but in general, this is resolved as that **whoever takes a Condition first loses the duel.**

Even though magic is on the table and authorities will not penalize you for hurting another student in a **duel,** hexes are still illegal in general and getting caught casting hexological magic will spell trouble. Lots of it. Could be worth winning the **duel**, though, really up to you.

Many spellcasters, especially evokers and **wandjocks** in general**,** have a strong culture of seeking **duels** with one another to see who is the greatest. It’s customary, but not required, to give a small magic token with a sigil unique to you to anyone who defeats you in a **duel,** and people seeking recognition and greatness in the field of magic **duels** will try to collect as many as possible from all different people.

People obsessed with winning as many **duels** as possible are often referred to as **duelbirds.** Some people take it as a compliment. Some people mean it as an insult. These two types of people often do not get along.

You cannot force someone into a **duel**—by nature, it’s only a **duel** if both parties agree to it and agree to the stakes beforehand. If you engage in a **duel** without the other person’s consent, that is not a **duel** but instead a different concept called **beating up some random innocent person,** which is actually frowned upon.

**Duels** are common to resolve disputes that don’t seem to have any other answer, to try to put down a rival, or just as a form of friendly competition between **duelbird** types. Keep in mind they don’t necessarily have to be brusque and violent—snatching someone’s wand from their hands and throwing it to the floor, or even just a little demonological persuasion to convince someone to fall, are all valid ways to win a **duel.**

## Daily Life

As a student at Starfall Academy, your highest commitment is to learning, to growth, to study, and to not casting tranquilizer spells on your professors.

Of course, you can’t always have it all.

Although inevitably you’ll be off on adventures plenty of times, you can’t spend every moment unraveling mysteries, dueling rival students, and surviving encounters with wights. You are still a student, and you will still have to attend to your studies.

It may sometimes be appropriate for the GM to track every hour through the day, but more often, it’s more convenient to break the day into three segments: morning, class time, and evening.

Morning is the time from sunrise until classes start, around 6-9am. During this time, typically, students go for breakfast in the Great Hall, but some may choose to go out to the cafes and restaurants in Starfall Citadel for some food on their own dollar (this system does not track money, but if you get a three-course meal for breakfast every morning and you don’t have rich parents, the GM might ask if you’re selling feet pics on the side).

Others still, of course, like to take advantage of the morning and students all being at the Great Hall for breakfast to go cause trouble.

Class time, of course, is when classes are in session, about 9am-5pm. Although they won’t be in class every minute of this time, students during this time period are generally focusing on their next class—getting their studies in, grabbing lunch with study partners, finishing assignments.

You could skip class to have this time for your own devices, but you won’t benefit from your classes as in the [Enrollment](#enrollment) section if you do, and if you repeatedly skip classes, you may start to get into trouble.

Evening runs after classes end, about 5pm until whenever you decide to sleep. During this time, you can either choose to actively play your character or perform [Downtime](#downtime)**.** If you actively play your character, the GM will run time as normal and you can go around campus and do as you like, and most other students will also be out attending to their own business in the evenings, and most campus events take place in the evenings or on the weekends.

Of course, weekends are where the magic happens. A weekend gives you two free blocks, allowing you to perform two **Downtime** activities or to perform a **Downtime** activity and still explore campus, or just spend extra time exploring campus.

At night, you sleep, which restores 1 Condition stack of your choice. You can choose to pull an all-nighter and stay awake, but you must **Resist, DC14** when you do, and you do not restore the Condition stack you normally would overnight. You cannot stay up two nights in a row, unless a specific effect states otherwise.

### Downtime

You can choose to spend a time block performing **Downtime,** which keeps your character busy with various activities to advance themselves, their studies, or other objectives that take time.

When you decide to perform **Downtime,** you can spend one time block—typically an evening—to select one option from the following list. On a weekend, you can devote your morning to a **Downtime** session, your evening to one, or do both, spending all day on **Downtime** and picking two options.

You can also choose to skip class on any weekday, treating it like a weekend. Some event days, such as the Old Saints’ Ball, have no classes and count as weekends, but if you attend the event, you cannot perform **Downtime** at the same time.

#### Downtime Activities

1. **Practice:** You can study magic or practice a skill, which will let you make an improvement roll in the given field.
2. **Research:** You can look into a certain subject. Tell the GM what you want to research, and the GM will tell you if the resources exist to research it and you have access to them. If they exist, the GM may ask you to make some check to represent getting access to the materials, but generally, you can just access them from libraries and class halls. Once you have access, make a **Research** check at a DC determined by the GM (generally 10-20), and the GM will give you one piece of information about what you’re researching per degree of success.
3. ****Study:** You can attempt to learn a spell, provided you have access to the materials and resources needed to learn it. Any one **Downtime** block counts as one day’s worth of study for spell-learning.**
4. ****Collect Materials:** You can spend your time gathering materials in a few ways.**
1. ****Gathering Plants:** You can search natural spaces for materials. Roll **Herbalism, DC5,** and take 150 materials per degree of success. You may encounter a creature, for better or worse.**
2. ****Doing Favors:** You can help people out in exchange for materials. Roll **Search, Improvise, Comprehend, Investigation,** or **Athletics**, **DC5,** and take 100 materials per degree of success. The GM may give you a small situation to resolve based on which ability you rolled, and you may strengthen your relationship with someone.**
3. ****Salvaging:** You can pick through scrap in manufacturing areas and garbage piles to gather materials. Roll **Improvise** or **Search**, **DC5,** and take 150 materials per degree of success. You might hurt yourself doing this—on a critical failure, you must **Resist Wound, DC14.****
4. ****Bartering:** You can bat your eyelashes at people and make deals to get your hands on materials. Roll **any Charm skill, DC5,** and take 100 materials per degree of success. If you roll with **Deception,** people may find out later that you lied and become angry, and if you roll with **Win Over,** you might end up with a new admirer.**
5. ****Stealing:** You can swipe some materials either from individuals or from Academy property. Roll **Stealth, DC5,** and take 200 materials per degree of success. On a critical failure, the GM will give you a situation to resolve as you risk getting caught.**
5. ****Connect:** You can improve your relationship with somebody. Roll **Win Over** at a DC determined by the GM based on how the person feels about you, generally 10-20, and on success, the person in question will like you better and be more willing to help you in the future or give you information you want.**
6. ****Rest:** You can spend your **Downtime** resting and clear a Condition stack. If you are **Metabolizing** a potion, take 1 day off the time remaining when you take this option.**
7. ****Crafting:** If you perform any crafting tasks such as brewing potions or artifact repair during **Downtime,** you can count one **Downtime** block as 8 hours. If you perform any checks for the crafting during this time, take a +4 to the check.**
8. ****Attune:** If you have an artifact you aren’t attuned to, you can spend your **Downtime** attempting to attune to it. Take a +4 to the **Attune** check, and you don’t need to **Resist** as a result of the attempt.**
9. ****Learn Glyphs:** You can spend your **Downtime** studying runic tomes and learn new glyphs. Roll **Runology, DC5,** and for each degree of success, you learn a new glyph of your choice and a random new glyph at the GM’s discretion.**
10. ****Study Recipes:** You can attempt to learn a new alchemical recipe. Spend the material cost of the potion whose recipe you want to learn, and roll **Alchemy** against its **Intensity Rank.** On success, you learn the recipe. On failure, your materials are refunded. On three or more degrees of success, you are on a roll: you can roll to learn a second alchemical recipe, so long as you spend the materials for that one as well. You cannot learn more than two recipes at once this way.**
11. ****Art:** Magic academies are places of art as much as they are of magic. If you have ranks in **Art,** decide what kind of artistic pursuit your character has and you can take this option in a variety of different ways.**
1. ****Perform:** Maybe you perform music or you put on magic displays or do a dozen things in between, but either way, you put on a show for an audience. Roll **Art, DC5,** and for each degree of success, you can choose to take 50 materials or influence one thing about your reputation, including simply becoming more famous. On 2 or more degrees of success, you instead take 100 materials per degree of success, take 150 per degree of success on 4 or more, and so forth.**
2. ****Contract Work:** Somebody specifically seeks your work and is willing to pay for it. Roll **Art, DC5,** and for each degree of success, take 100 materials and improve your relationship with somebody connected to the work, such as the client, a fellow performer, somebody else at a venue you’re contracted to perform, etc.**
3. ****Street Art:** Graffiti, underground performances, flash mobs, and impromptu stumping are all the category here: guerilla art with a cause that moves people. Roll **Art, DC10,** and for each degree of success, you can push one idea out to a group or a niche community of your choice, or you can spend three degrees of success to push an idea out to the general populace. This won’t necessarily convince people of the idea, but it will put the idea into the spotlight, potentially damaging or clearing somebody’s reputation. On a critical failure, you get in trouble with the academy, or the GM may have you resolve a situation where you risk getting in trouble.**
4. ****Collective:** You can gather other artists for an art collective, such as a showmagic workshop, a group jam, writing workshop, rap cypher, painters’ group meet, etc. Roll **Art, DC5.** For each degree of success, you can improve a connection between any two people (including yourself) who might have come to the collective, you can gain insight into a situation from somebody at the collective, you can learn something about a person in the collective, or you can choose somebody (including yourself) to make an improvement roll in **Art**. A given person can only make one improvement roll from each **Collective.****
5. ****Sell Out:** It might make you less cool to make art for the highest bidder, the lowest common denominator, or the biggest authority, but some materials lining your pockets takes the sting out. Roll **Art, DC5,** and take 150 materials per degree of success. Choose a group to worsen your reputation with when you take this option.**
6. ****Magnum Opus:** You can strive down a long road towards a great piece. Note down what your **Magnum Opus** is, and roll **Art, DC5,** tracking how many degrees of success you get. You can perform this **Downtime** option multiple times, counting up the degrees of success, until you accumulate 20, at which point you complete the **Magnum Opus.** Once you do, take 2000 materials and work with the GM to decide what dramatic effect creating the **Magnum Opus** has on your life—perhaps it’s propelled you to stardom, or perhaps just fifteen minutes of fame, perhaps it’s changed the way people look at something or someone, perhaps it’s led you to a great bounty like even more materials or the learning materials for a Legendary spell, or perhaps even to a great bounty at a cost of a dark reputation. You can start a new **Magnum Opus** as soon as you’ve finished one, if you’re itching for the next hit of stardom.**

If you want to perform anything that feels like an equivalent **Downtime** activity that isn’t on this list, work with the GM and work out the details of what it looks like for you.

### Enrollment

During any given semester, you have 4 courses you’re taking. Choose 4 at the start of the campaign, and every new semester, you can choose 4 new classes, but for each major subject you have, you must take at least 1 class in that subject and at least 1 more class in the same category.

For example, a student majoring in **Alchemy** will need to take at least 1 **Alchemy** class and then at least 1 other class in Creation Magics (Alchemy, Artificy, Enchantment, Herbalism, Runology, Wandcrafting). A second **Alchemy** class will fulfill both requirements.

A student majoring in **Alchemy** and **Herbalism,** meanwhile, must take a class in **Alchemy** and a class in **Herbalism**, but then all requirements are fulfilled, because **Alchemy** and **Herbalism** are both in the same category. A student majoring in **Alchemy** and **Arcane History,** however, must take 1 class in **Alchemy** and 1 more in Creation Magics, and 1 in **Arcane History** and 1 more in Wisdom Magics.

In this way, it is possible for a student to have 3 or even 4 majors, as long as they are all in the same category, but this is not recommended.

If you’re a stickler for details, you can work out an exact timetable for your classes with the GM for granular control over playing every hour of the day, but generally, it’s assumed that all your classes fall into the regular “Class Time” block in the middle of every day (described in the header section of [Daily Life](#daily-life)).

If you attend your classes as regular, then at the end of class time, the GM may pick one of the following options:

#### Class Benefits

1. ****Subject Improvement:** The GM will pick one of your class subjects for you to make an improvement roll in.**
2. ****New Spell Materials:** You obtain the resources and materials needed to learn a new spell. You will still have to practice and study to learn the spell. If you have 2 or more classes in the relevant subject, you may obtain the instructional materials for advanced spells this way.**
3. ****Inspiration:** The GM will pick a subject you’re studying, and you can take a +4 bonus to that subject until the end of the day.**
4. ****Relevant Information:** The GM gives you a piece of information relevant to an objective you’re undertaking, be it from study materials or classroom gossip.**
5. ****Connections:** You improve your relationship with a classmate or a professor in one of your classes, or make a new connection with somebody in one of your classes.**
6. ****Spell Learning:** The GM will pick a spell you’re learning in the same field as one of your classes for you to make a spell-learning roll in. This roll doesn’t cost materials.**
7. ****Recipes:** You obtain a recipe for a potion, new glyphs, or even the schema for an artifact.**
8. ****Materials:** You obtain some materials, typically 300-700 at the GM’s discretion.**

### Learning Spells

Spells are integral to any character in Starfall Academy, and one of the most important components of character growth is learning new spells, giving new abilities and techniques.

Learning any new spell requires the materials—instructional materials, as well as magic materials to practice casting it—and practice time. Basic and Standard spells are significantly easier to find the materials for and learn than Advanced spells, which are significantly easier than Legendary spells or Hexes.

When you set out to learn a spell, offer up **200 materials** upfront and then spend either **8 hours**, nonconsecutively, or **1 Downtime** **block** studying it. At the end, roll the relevant Subject against the DC listed in the table below, and on success, you finish 1 day of progress towards learning the spell. If you fail, the materials are refunded.

Instructional materials for Basic spells can be found with no effort. They are freely available not only in any library but even on the internet.

The instructional materials for Standard spells can typically be found in public-access archives and halls in any magic academy, but the GM may rule that any given standard spell may not be so easy to find. If the GM rules it is available, spend 4 hours or one **Downtime** block tracking the information down, and make a **Research** or **Recall Information** check at a DC determined by the GM (usually 10-20). On success, you obtain the instructional materials and can work on learning the spell.

The instructional materials for Advanced spells are a different story. These are often proprietary and closely guarded by their creators, which can be individual inventors or corporate entities. You will not be able to track down the instructional materials in the same way as with Basic or Standard spells, but you can spend 4 hours or one **Downtime** block looking for them, making a **Research** check at a DC determined by the GM (usually 10-20), which will give clues as to how you might be able to get your hands on the instructional materials.

These are often learned through classes or given as rewards for quest progress. For example, Carlos, Maya and Finn finally track down a secret hideout for the corrupt chronomancy professor, and they go through his scribbled journals and find his writings on the advanced **Chronomancy** spell **Between the Clock Hands,** meaning everyone in the party now has access to the instructional materials if they wish to learn the spell.

Theoretically, you could take these instructional materials and charge other students to use them. However, the majority of these spells are copyrighted intellectual property, meaning you can get in a lot of trouble doing this.

Hexes and Legendary spells both have instructional materials that can never be found through normal means. It takes extraordinary circumstances to find these, and the very possibility to obtain them usually represents some remarkable plot points. The GM may always give the instructional materials for a Hex or Legendary spell, but don’t expect to just stumble upon them.

If you have somebody who already knows a given spell help you learn it, you can take a +4 to any checks to learn it.
